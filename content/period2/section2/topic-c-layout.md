# Layout

Layout is where fabrication becomes real. The drawing says where things go; layout is the act of *marking it onto the metal* so you can cut, drill, and weld with confidence. A layout error is a fabrication error — and unlike a calculation error on paper, a layout error costs steel. Get the process systematic and you won't repeat it.

---

## Layout tools: what each one is for

A welder's layout toolkit is different from a carpenter's. Steel doesn't absorb pencil lines, and the marks must survive grinding, handling, and heat.[^1][^3]

| Tool | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Steel rule / tape measure** | Linear measurements | Imperial (fractional or decimal) or metric — never mix systems on one job |
| **Combination square** | 90° and 45° lines; depth gauge; height gauge | The most versatile layout tool in the shop |
| **Try square (machinist's square)** | Verify 90° — checking squareness of cut edges | More precise than combination square for verification |
| **Bevel protractor (bevel gauge)** | Transfer and verify angles other than 90° | Lock and transfer bevel angle to material |
| **Centre punch** | Mark centre of holes before drilling | Short, sharp tap with hammer; use prick punch first for accuracy |
| **Prick punch** | Light, fine starting mark before the centre punch | Sharper angle than centre punch — for precise location first |
| **Compass (trammel points on beam)** | Scribe arcs and circles; step off equal distances | Beam trammel for large radii |
| **Dividers** | Step off equal spacing along a line; transfer measurements | Two sharp points; used like a compass but for distances not circles |
| **Scriber** | Scratch a line into metal surface | Hardened point; leaves a mark that survives heat and abrasion |
| **Soapstone** | Mark lines on steel for cutting and welding | Soft, white; line burns off during welding; leaves no permanent mark |
| **Layout dye (layout fluid)** | Coat metal surface bright blue to make scribed lines highly visible | Applied by brush or spray; scriber lines show bright silver against blue |
| **Chalk line** | Snap long straight lines on plate | Use chalk not dye for this — snap a straight datum line over long distances |
| **Magnetic protractor** | Reads angles directly on steel using a magnetic base | Useful for checking bevel angles on cut plate |

### Layout dye procedure:
1. Clean the metal surface (degrease — wipe with acetone)
2. Apply layout dye with brush or aerosol — thin, even coat
3. Allow to dry (typically 30–60 seconds)
4. Scribe your lines with a hardened scriber
5. The scribed lines appear as bright silver against the blue background

## Templates: what they are and when to use them

A **template** is a physical pattern — cut from thin steel, polycarbonate, cardboard, or plywood — that matches the shape of a part exactly. It's used to mark repetitive parts without measuring from scratch each time.[^1][^3]

**When templates pay off:**
- Same part needed 5 or more times
- Complex curved or compound shapes that are hard to lay out by dimension
- Hole patterns that must align perfectly across multiple parts
- Saddle cuts (fish-mouth cuts) for branch pipe connections

**Template materials:**
- **Thin sheet steel (16–20 gauge):** durable; survives shop handling; used for long production runs
- **Polycarbonate / plexiglass:** easy to cut with a jigsaw; can be written on; for medium runs
- **Cardboard or Masonite:** quick first-run template; not for production

**Template construction procedure:**[^1]
1. Draw or print the part shape full-scale on paper
2. Transfer to template material (trace outline, punch centre marks through)
3. Cut the template — preserve the outside edge precisely (inside scrap can be rougher)
4. Test-fit against one part before committing to production: place on plate, trace, cut one piece, verify fit/dimensions
5. Mark the template with: part number, revision, date, which side is "up" or "face side"
6. Store templates flat — hanging or stacking causes warping

### Saddle (fish-mouth) template for pipe fitting
When a branch pipe meets a run pipe at an angle, the branch end must be cut to a curved profile (saddle) to fit against the round run pipe surface.[^1]

**Simple saddle template construction (same diameter branch and run, 90° branch):**
1. Divide the branch pipe circumference into equal segments (12 divisions)
2. Mark each segment's height from the cut end using a wrap-around template from trigonometric calculation
3. Connect points — this gives the saddle cut line
4. The wrapped template is applied around the branch pipe and the saddle is cut to the line

## Plate utilization: getting the most from your stock

**Plate utilization** (also called nesting) is the process of arranging parts on available plate to minimize scrap.[^1]

### Factors in utilization decisions:
- **Material cost:** Wasted plate = wasted money. Plate steel costs typically by the kg — minimize cut waste.
- **Grain direction:** Some applications require cuts parallel to (or perpendicular to) the rolling direction for impact toughness. Know your WPS or material spec.
- **Kerf width:** OFC kerf is typically 1.5–4 mm wide — leave a gap between adjacent parts for the kerf. For plasma: 1–2 mm. For shear: essentially zero.
- **Edge preparation:** If parts need bevels after cutting, allow for grinding/cutting access around each part.
- **Marking order:** Mark parts from one corner of the plate, working systematically — don't scatter marks across the whole plate before cutting.

### Plate nesting — best practices:[^1]
1. List all parts by dimensions (largest first)
2. Start with the largest part — place it in a corner of the plate
3. Fill in smaller parts around the large one
4. Maintain at least 3 mm between parts for kerf + grinding clearance
5. Align parts with the long dimension of the plate where possible (reduces handling difficulty)
6. Calculate utilization rate: (total part area ÷ plate area) × 100% — target >85% for efficient nesting
7. Mark all pieces with part number before cutting to avoid mix-up

## Pipe sizes and schedules

Understanding pipe sizing is essential for layout of pipe-to-pipe fits and nozzle connections.[^4]

### Nominal Pipe Size (NPS) vs actual diameter

**Critical fact:** The NPS is NOT the actual outside diameter (OD) for most sizes below NPS 14.[^4]

| NPS | Actual OD | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1/2" | 21.3 mm (0.840") | |
| 1" | 33.4 mm (1.315") | |
| 2" | 60.3 mm (2.375") | |
| 4" | 114.3 mm (4.500") | |
| 6" | 168.3 mm (6.625") | |
| 8" | 219.1 mm (8.625") | |
| 12" | 323.9 mm (12.750") | |
| 14" | 355.6 mm (14.000") — OD = NPS from here up | |

**Above NPS 14, OD = NPS exactly.** Below NPS 14, the OD is larger than the NPS number — a historical convention from gas pipe era.

### Schedule (wall thickness)

The schedule designation indicates wall thickness. Same OD, different schedule = different ID and different wall.[^4]

| NPS | Schedule 40 wall | Schedule 80 wall | Schedule 160 wall |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2" | 3.91 mm | 5.54 mm | 8.71 mm |
| 4" | 6.02 mm | 8.56 mm | 13.49 mm |
| 6" | 7.11 mm | 10.97 mm | 18.26 mm |
| 8" | 8.18 mm | 12.70 mm | 23.01 mm |

*Standard (STD) = Schedule 40 for NPS 1/8" to 10"; XS (Extra Strong) = Schedule 80; XXS (Double Extra Strong) varies.*

**Pipe ID = OD − (2 × wall thickness)**

**Why schedule matters for layout:** When cutting a branch saddle, you need the *actual* OD of both the branch and run pipes. Using NPS instead of actual OD produces an incorrectly-sized saddle template.

## Numbers you need to memorize

- **NPS 4 actual OD:** 114.3 mm (4.500")[^4]
- **NPS 6 actual OD:** 168.3 mm (6.625")[^4]
- **NPS 14 and above: OD = NPS** (no correction needed)[^4]
- **Kerf allowance (OFC on plate):** 1.5–4 mm — allow between adjacent parts[^1]
- **Plate utilization target:** >85% to be considered efficient nesting[^1]
- **Layout dye:** apply to degreased surface; scribe lines appear bright against blue[^1]
- **Prick punch before centre punch** — fine location mark first, then heavier centre punch to guide drill[^1]

---

## What the textbook doesn't tell you

**The NPS-to-OD disconnect trips up pipe fitters and welders constantly.** If you're designing a saddle cut for a 4" NPS branch on a 6" NPS run, you don't draw a 4-inch radius circle and a 6-inch circle — you use 114.3 mm OD and 168.3 mm OD. If you use the NPS numbers directly, your template will be wrong. The pipes won't fit.

**Templates should be tested before production commitment.** Cut one part from the template. Fit it into the actual assembly. If it's wrong, you've lost one piece of scrap. If you cut 50 parts before checking, you've lost 50 pieces plus the time. This sounds obvious but gets skipped constantly under time pressure.

**Layout dye is optional but not optional on thick plate.** On thin gauge, a soapstone line is fine. On 25 mm plate where a misread line costs $200 in steel, the layout dye plus scriber line takes 2 minutes and is unambiguous. Use it.

**Chalk lines for long datum lines.** If you're laying out a 6-metre plate for structural members, you can't hold a tape measure straight enough. Snap a chalk line first to establish a straight baseline along one long edge. All measurements come off the chalk line — not from a wandering tape.

## Key terms

- **Layout:** process of transferring dimensions from a drawing to physical material using marking tools
- **Scriber:** hardened steel point used to scratch fine lines into metal for accurate layout
- **Layout dye (layout fluid):** bright blue fluid applied to metal surfaces to make scribed lines clearly visible
- **Soapstone:** white marking stick for temporary lines on steel — burns off during welding
- **Centre punch:** conical tool used to create a dimple at a marked point to guide a drill
- **Prick punch:** fine-pointed tool for initial location marks before centre punching
- **Template:** physical pattern made from thin material used to transfer the outline of a repetitive part
- **Saddle cut:** curved cut on the end of a branch pipe to fit against the curved surface of a run pipe
- **Plate utilization:** efficiency of part nesting on available plate — (part area / plate area) × 100%
- **Nesting:** arranging parts on plate to maximize utilization and minimize waste
- **NPS (Nominal Pipe Size):** the identifying number for pipe — NOT the actual outside diameter (except ≥ NPS 14)
- **Schedule (pipe):** designation indicating pipe wall thickness; higher schedule = thicker wall = smaller ID for same OD

---

## Common exam trap

- **NPS ≠ actual pipe OD for sizes below NPS 14** — this is the most common pipe sizing error. NPS 4 OD = 114.3 mm, NOT 4 inches (101.6 mm).
- **Schedule 80 has THICKER walls than Schedule 40** — same OD, less ID, more pressure capacity. Exam may imply higher schedule = larger pipe. Wrong — it's just thicker walls.
- **Prick punch before centre punch — not the reverse** — prick punch for accuracy of location; centre punch for drill guidance. Do them in order.
- **Plate utilization <85% is generally considered poor** — if an exam asks what's acceptable, >85% is the standard benchmark.
- **Soapstone burns off during welding** — it's appropriate for marking weld sequences and cut lines. Layout dye + scriber is for precise layout lines that need to survive handling, not welding heat.

## Practice question preview

**Q:** A welder needs to lay out a branch saddle connection where a 4" NPS Schedule 40 branch pipe meets a 6" NPS Schedule 40 run pipe at 90°. What outside diameter dimensions should be used for the layout template?

A) 4" branch OD and 6" run OD
B) 101.6 mm branch OD and 152.4 mm run OD
C) 114.3 mm branch OD and 168.3 mm run OD
D) 114.3 mm branch OD and 152.4 mm run OD

**Correct: C**

**Explanation:** NPS 4 pipe has an actual outside diameter of 114.3 mm (4.500"), and NPS 6 pipe has an actual OD of 168.3 mm (6.625") per ASME B36.10M. The NPS designation does not equal actual OD for pipe sizes below NPS 14. Using the NPS numbers in inches (A) or their inch-to-mm conversion (B: 4"=101.6mm, 6"=152.4mm) would produce incorrect saddle geometry. Wall thickness (schedule) affects only ID and thus the depth of fit, not the OD for the template.
