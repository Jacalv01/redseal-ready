# GTAW Electrodes, Filler Metals and Shielding Gases

The three consumables in GTAW — tungsten, filler rod, and shielding gas — must be matched to each other and to the base metal. Get any one wrong and you'll spend your session troubleshooting contamination, porosity, or arc instability. Get all three right and GTAW is the cleanest, most satisfying arc process there is.

---

## Tungsten electrodes: types and colours

Tungsten electrodes are classified by AWS A5.12. The classification code tells you what additives are in the tungsten — these additives affect arc starting, arc stability, and how well the tungsten holds up to the current type being used.[^1][^6]

### Complete electrode type table

| AWS class | Common name | Colour band | Additive | Current type | Best materials |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **EWTh-2** | 2% thoriated | **Red** | 2% ThO₂ | DCEN | Steel, stainless, titanium, copper |
| **EWTh-1** | 1% thoriated | Yellow | 1% ThO₂ | DCEN | Steel, stainless |
| **EWCe-2** | 2% ceriated | **Grey** | 2% CeO₂ | DCEN or AC | Steel, stainless, aluminum |
| **EWLa-1.5** | 1.5% lanthanated | **Gold** | 1.5% La₂O₃ | DCEN or AC | Steel, stainless, aluminum |
| **EWZr-8** | 0.8% zirconiated | **White** | 0.8% ZrO₂ | AC | Aluminum, magnesium |
| **EWP** | Pure tungsten | **Green** | None | AC | Aluminum, magnesium |

### Key characteristics:

**EWTh-2 (2% thoriated, red):**
- The most common DCEN electrode in North American shops
- Excellent arc stability and long electrode life on DC
- Radiation consideration: thorium is mildly radioactive — grinding thoriated electrodes produces fine dust that must not be inhaled. Use a wet grinder or purpose-made tungsten grinder with dust capture. Dispose of grinding debris per your shop's hazardous waste procedure.
- Does NOT ball on DCEN — stays pointed or slightly rounded tip

**EWCe-2 (2% ceriated, grey) and EWLa-1.5 (1.5% lanthanated, gold):**
- Non-radioactive alternatives to thoriated — preferred where radiation handling is a concern
- Very similar arc characteristics to thoriated on DCEN
- Work on both DC and AC — flexible for shops that weld both steel and aluminum

**EWZr-8 (0.8% zirconiated, white) and EWP (pure tungsten, green):**
- Designed for AC aluminum welding — form a stable molten ball at the tip during AC operation
- The ball forms because AC's electrode-positive half-cycle heats the tip significantly
- EWZr-8 forms a cleaner, more stable ball than EWP — generally preferred over pure tungsten for AC aluminum
- Do NOT use on DCEN — they overheat and split on DC

## Electrode preparation: pointed vs balled

The tip geometry must match the current type.[^1][^6]

### DCEN: pointed tip

- **Grind the tungsten to a taper** — the cone angle affects arc behaviour
- **Fine point (10–15° included angle):** narrow, focused arc; good for thin material and tight joints
- **Blunter taper (20–30° included angle):** slightly wider arc; more stable for thicker material
- **Grinding direction:** always grind longitudinally (along the axis), not circumferentially. Circumferential grinding leaves radial grooves → the arc rotates around the tip instead of focusing at the point → erratic arc start, wandering arc during welding[^6]
- **Grind with tungsten rotating slowly** — a grinding wheel dedicated to tungsten only (cross-contamination with steel or aluminum will contaminate the tungsten)

### AC: balled tip

- Do NOT point the tungsten for AC aluminum — the electrode-positive half-cycle will melt the point and form a ball anyway. Let the ball form naturally.
- To establish the ball: arc up on a copper or scrap aluminum plate at the desired amperage; the ball will form in 5–15 seconds; then proceed to weld
- **Ball diameter** should not exceed 1.5× the tungsten diameter. Larger balls are unstable and may fall off into the weld pool.
- If the ball forms unevenly or spits during setup: contaminated tungsten — break off the tip and reform the ball

### Electrode diameter selection

| GTAW current (A) | Recommended tungsten diameter |
|---|---|
| < 80 A | 1.6 mm (1/16") |
| 80–150 A | 2.4 mm (3/32") |
| 150–250 A | 3.2 mm (1/8") |
| 250–400 A | 4.0 mm (5/32") or 4.8 mm (3/16") |

## Filler metals for GTAW

Filler rods for GTAW are solid bare rods, typically cut to 915 mm (36") lengths and bundled in a tube. They are NOT coated like SMAW electrodes.[^2][^3][^4]

### Mild steel filler (AWS A5.18)[^2]

| Classification | Si content | Mn content | Deoxidizers | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ER70S-2** | 0.50% | 1.40% | Yes (Ti, Al, Zr added) | Pipe root passes; dirty or rusty steel; any critical pipe weld |
| **ER70S-3** | 0.45–0.75% | 0.90–1.40% | Minimal | Clean steel only; general shop GTAW |
| **ER70S-6** | 0.80–1.15% | 1.40–1.85% | Yes (high Mn+Si) | Mill scale, slightly rusty or less-than-perfect steel |

**ER70S-2 is the preferred filler for critical GTAW work on pipe** — its triple deoxidizer package (titanium + aluminum + zirconium) scavenges any oxygen or nitrogen contamination from the weld pool, producing the cleanest possible weld. Used for root passes on ASME and pipeline code work.[^2]

**ER70S-6** has the highest Mn + Si deoxidizer content — it tolerates slightly less clean base metal better than ER70S-2 or ER70S-3. Used in general shop GTAW on hot-rolled plate with light scale.

### Stainless steel filler (AWS A5.9)[^3]

| Classification | Carbon content | Alloy | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ER308L** | <0.03% C ("L" = low carbon) | 18Cr-8Ni | Welding 304L and 308L stainless — general purpose; food service equipment |
| **ER316L** | <0.03% C | 16Cr-12Ni-2Mo | Welding 316L stainless — marine, chemical, food industries; better pitting corrosion resistance |
| **ER309L** | <0.03% C | 24Cr-12Ni | Dissimilar welds: stainless to carbon steel; buffer layer on carbon steel before 308 overlay |
| **ER310** | Varied | 25Cr-20Ni | High-temperature service (furnace components, >900°C) |

**"L" grades are preferred for GTAW** because the low carbon content prevents carbide sensitization in the HAZ (chromium carbide precipitation at grain boundaries that causes intergranular corrosion — called "weld decay").[^3]

### Aluminum filler (AWS A5.10)[^4]

(Covered in detail in Section 1 Topic F — summary here)

| Filler | Best base metal | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **ER4043** | 6061, 6063, castings | Lower cracking susceptibility; good fluidity; turns grey when anodized |
| **ER5356** | 5052, 5083, 6061 (color match) | Higher strength; good corrosion resistance; use below 65°C service |

## Shielding gases for GTAW

GTAW uses **only inert gases** — no active gas (CO₂, O₂) which would contaminate the tungsten and weld pool.[^5]

### Argon (100% Ar) — the universal GTAW gas[^5]

**Why argon is the default:**
- Inert — does not react with tungsten or weld pool
- Denser than air (density 1.78 kg/m³ vs 1.29 kg/m³ for air) — settles down over the weld pool for effective protection
- Good arc starting and stability characteristics
- Available in high purity grades (99.995%+)

**Purity requirement:** GTAW requires a minimum of 99.995% pure argon (Grade 4.5 or better)[^5] — any oxygen or nitrogen contamination appears as porosity and tungsten contamination. Use welding-grade argon, not industrial-grade (which may have higher impurities).

**Applications:** All metals — mild steel, stainless, aluminum, titanium, copper, nickel alloys

### Argon-Helium mixtures (Ar/He)[^5]

**Why add helium:**
- Helium produces a hotter arc than argon — helium has higher thermal conductivity, transferring more heat to the workpiece
- Result: **deeper penetration** and faster travel speed for a given amperage
- Useful for thick copper (needs high heat input due to thermal conductivity) and thick aluminum

**Disadvantages:**
- Helium is less dense than air — requires higher flow rates to achieve good coverage
- Expensive — helium costs significantly more than argon per unit volume
- Arc starting is more difficult with high helium content

**Common mixtures:** 25% He / 75% Ar (moderate effect); 50/50 (significant penetration increase); 75% He / 25% Ar (high heat, requires higher flow rate)

### Argon-Hydrogen (Ar/H₂) — austenitic stainless steel only[^5]

**Why add hydrogen (limited applications):**
- Hydrogen addition (typically 2–5% H₂) increases arc temperature and cleaning action
- Result: faster travel speed on thin stainless steel, especially tubing
- Produces a slightly reducing atmosphere that prevents oxidation on the weld surface

**CRITICAL RESTRICTION:** Ar/H₂ MUST NOT be used on:[^5]
- Carbon steel or low-alloy steel — hydrogen causes HIC (hydrogen-induced cracking)
- Aluminum — hydrogen causes porosity (aluminum readily absorbs hydrogen)
- Nickel alloys with high sulfur content — can cause hot cracking
- **Only suitable for:** austenitic stainless steel (304, 316, 321 family)

**Common mixtures:** Ar + 2% H₂; Ar + 5% H₂ (maximum for most applications)

## Care of filler rods and tungsten

Filler rods and tungsten must be kept clean to prevent contamination.[^6]

**Filler rod handling:**
- Store in original tube or a clean, dry, sealed container — do not leave on the bench exposed to shop air, grease, or moisture
- Handle only with clean gloves — skin oils contaminate the rod end and produce porosity
- Wipe rod end with acetone before use (especially aluminum filler — oxide reforms quickly)
- Do NOT draw the rod through your fingers along its length — this deposits oils from skin

**Tungsten handling:**
- Store in a case or tube; don't let tips contact dirty surfaces
- Never use tungsten that has touched the weld pool (contaminated) without re-grinding the tip
- For thoriated tungsten: use a wet grinder or dedicated tungsten grinder; capture grinding debris; follow your shop's radiation waste protocol

## Numbers you need to memorize

- **EWTh-2 (red):** 2% thoriated, DCEN, steel/stainless[^1]
- **EWCe-2 (grey):** 2% ceriated, DCEN or AC, versatile[^1]
- **EWLa-1.5 (gold):** 1.5% lanthanated, DCEN or AC, non-radioactive[^1]
- **EWZr-8 (white) and EWP (green):** AC aluminum, ball tip[^1]
- **Tungsten grinding direction:** longitudinal (along the axis), not circumferential[^6]
- **DCEN tip:** pointed; AC tip: balled (≤ 1.5× tungsten diameter)[^1][^6]
- **ER70S-2:** triple deoxidizer (Ti, Al, Zr); best for pipe root on rusty/dirty steel[^2]
- **ER308L:** low-carbon 18-8 stainless filler; prevents carbide sensitization[^3]
- **ER316L:** low-carbon 16-12-2 stainless; molybdenum adds pitting corrosion resistance[^3]
- **Ar/H₂:** ONLY for austenitic stainless — never on carbon steel or aluminum[^5]
- **GTAW argon purity requirement:** ≥99.995% (Grade 4.5 or better)[^5]

## What the textbook doesn't tell you

**Thoriated tungsten is the workhorse, but ceriated or lanthanated is the future.** Some shops and sites are moving away from thoriated to eliminate the radiation handling requirement — particularly in fabrication shops with high tungsten grinding rates (welding stainless daily). EWCe-2 and EWLa-1.5 perform nearly identically to EWTh-2 on DCEN and also work on AC — versatile in a way that thoriated isn't. Learn the colour codes; you'll be asked to pick the right one in your first year of work.

**Drawing the filler rod in at the wrong angle or wrong speed is the biggest beginner mistake in GTAW.** The rod should be fed into the leading edge of the puddle at a shallow angle (15-20° from horizontal) in small, rhythmic dips. Too steep and you stab the tungsten; too slow and you lose the weld pool before the filler makes contact. The rhythm is: torch moves, puddle forms, dip rod in, withdraw rod slightly, torch moves again. It takes weeks to make this feel natural.

**Ar/H₂ gas is dramatically more productive on thin stainless tube** — a common application in pharmaceutical and food-grade piping. Travel speed on 3 mm wall stainless tube can be 30-50% faster with Ar/5%H₂ than pure argon, with a cleaner back-bead. But if anyone uses that same gas cylinder on a carbon steel repair weld, hydrogen cracking can show up days later. Label Ar/H₂ cylinders clearly and lock them to stainless applications only.

## Key terms

- **EWTh-2:** 2% thoriated tungsten electrode; red colour; DCEN; mild radioactivity from thorium
- **EWCe-2:** 2% ceriated tungsten; grey; non-radioactive; DCEN or AC; versatile
- **EWLa-1.5:** 1.5% lanthanated tungsten; gold; non-radioactive; DCEN or AC
- **EWZr-8:** 0.8% zirconiated tungsten; white; AC aluminum welding; stable ball tip
- **EWP:** pure tungsten; green; AC aluminum; standard ball formation
- **Balled tip:** rounded tungsten tip formed during AC operation by the electrode-positive half-cycle heating
- **Pointed tip:** ground conical tungsten tip for DCEN welding; narrow, focused arc
- **ER70S-2:** mild steel GTAW filler with triple deoxidizers (Ti, Al, Zr); best for critical pipe root passes
- **ER70S-6:** high Mn+Si mild steel filler; better tolerance for scale or slightly contaminated steel
- **ER308L:** low-carbon 18Cr-8Ni stainless filler; prevents sensitization; general-purpose stainless GTAW
- **ER316L:** low-carbon 16Cr-12Ni-2Mo stainless filler; improved corrosion resistance (Mo addition)
- **Sensitization (weld decay):** chromium carbide precipitation in HAZ of stainless steel during welding; causes intergranular corrosion; prevented by using "L" (low carbon) grades
- **Argon Grade 4.5:** 99.995% purity argon — minimum for GTAW shielding
- **Ar/H₂:** argon-hydrogen mix for austenitic stainless GTAW only — causes HIC on carbon steel

## Common exam trap

- **EWTh-2 is RED; EWP is GREEN** — colour codes are heavily tested. Don't rely on memory alone: when in doubt, the AWS A5.12 colour-coded bands on the electrode end tell you definitively.
- **Pure tungsten (EWP, green) and zirconiated (EWZr-8, white) are for AC** — not DCEN. On DCEN they overheat and split. The exam may describe a DCEN steel application and offer EWP as an option.
- **Longitudinal grinding = correct; circumferential grinding = wrong** — the question will describe a grinding method and ask if it's correct or what the result will be. Circumferential grooves → wandering arc.
- **Ar/H₂ is ONLY for austenitic stainless** — using it on carbon steel causes hydrogen-induced cracking. This is the most dangerous wrong choice on a shielding gas question.
- **ER70S-2 is preferred for pipe root on potentially dirty steel** — not ER70S-6 (which is for plate GMAW on scale) or ER70S-3 (clean plate only).
- **"L" grade stainless filler prevents sensitization** — the exam may ask why ER308L is specified instead of ER308. The answer is carbon control — low carbon prevents chromium carbide formation.

## Practice question preview

**Q:** A welder is setting up for GTAW on 316L austenitic stainless steel pipe. The WPS calls for 100% argon shielding at 12 L/min. A co-worker suggests switching to Ar/5%H₂ shielding gas to increase travel speed. Which of the following correctly describes the acceptable use of Ar/H₂ shielding gas?

A) Ar/H₂ is acceptable on any stainless steel and increases travel speed — the suggestion is correct
B) Ar/H₂ is acceptable ONLY on austenitic stainless steels; for 316L, verify the WPS allows it
C) Ar/H₂ causes hydrogen cracking in stainless steel and must never be used on it
D) Ar/H₂ is acceptable on all metals including carbon steel and aluminum because argon is the dominant component

**Correct: B**

**Explanation:** Ar/H₂ shielding gas is technically suitable for austenitic stainless steels (304, 316, 321 family) — the hydrogen addition increases arc temperature and travel speed without causing hydrogen-induced cracking, since austenitic stainless has much lower susceptibility to HIC than carbon steel. However, the WPS must be followed — if the WPS says 100% Ar, the welder cannot substitute Ar/H₂ without re-qualifying the procedure. (A) Is partially correct but omits the WPS restriction. (C) Ar/H₂ does NOT cause HIC in austenitic stainless — this is specifically the one alloy family where it can be used. (D) Ar/H₂ causes HIC in carbon steel and porosity in aluminum — it is NOT suitable for those materials.
