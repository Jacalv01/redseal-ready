# Oxyfuel Cutting on Mild Steel Plate

Oxyfuel cutting is controlled burning. The steel does the cutting — the oxygen is just the oxidizer. In Period 2 you push beyond straight-line cuts into practical shop geometry: piercing holes in plate, cutting elliptical openings for penetrations, coping structural shapes to fit, and producing bevels for weld preparation. Get these right and you're doing real fabrication work.

---

## Recap: how oxyfuel cutting works

Before going to advanced technique, make sure the fundamentals are solid.[^3]

**The chemical reaction:**
1. Preheat flame brings the steel to its ignition temperature — approximately **870°C (1600°F)** for mild steel[^3]
2. Cutting oxygen stream turns the heated steel into iron oxide (Fe₂O₃/Fe₃O₄) — a liquid slag
3. The high-velocity oxygen jet blows the slag out of the kerf
4. Fresh steel on the kerf face ignites and the reaction is self-sustaining as the torch moves

**Critical condition:** The steel must be at ignition temperature *before* the cutting oxygen opens. If not, the oxygen stream cools the metal and the cut extinguishes. This is why preheat is not optional — it's chemistry.

**Mild steel cuts cleanly; cast iron and stainless do NOT** — their oxides are higher melting point than the base metal, so the slag can't blow clear. Oxyfuel cutting is only appropriate for mild and low-alloy steels.[^1]

## Equipment and tip selection

| Plate thickness | Tip number (typical, varies by brand) | Cutting O₂ pressure (kPa) | Preheat O₂ pressure (kPa) | Fuel gas pressure (kPa) | Travel speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3–6 mm | 0 or 00 | 275–345 kPa (40–50 psi) | 55–70 kPa | 35–55 kPa | Fast (~500–600 mm/min) |
| 6–12 mm | 1 | 345–415 kPa (50–60 psi) | 55–70 kPa | 35–55 kPa | Medium |
| 12–25 mm | 2 | 415–480 kPa (60–70 psi) | 70–105 kPa | 35–55 kPa | Medium-slow |
| 25–50 mm | 3 | 480–550 kPa (70–80 psi) | 105–140 kPa | 55–70 kPa | Slow |

*Always verify pressures against your equipment manufacturer's chart for your specific tip series.*[^1]

**Tip condition is everything:** A clogged or damaged tip port produces a crooked, rough, or extinguishing cut. Clean tips with the correct-size tip cleaner (never a drill bit — it reams the bore and ruins the tip geometry). Replace tips with deformed ports.[^1]

## Piercing technique (step-by-step)

Piercing — starting a cut in the middle of a plate — is the skill that unlocks access holes, structural penetrations, manhole openings, and all interior shape cuts.[^1][^3]

**Why piercing is different from edge starting:**
When you start at an edge, slag falls away freely. When you pierce in the middle, slag and sparks blow back toward the tip — risk of tip clogging or backfire if done wrong.

### Step-by-step: pierce on 10 mm (3/8") mild steel plate

1. **Select the correct tip** — typically a #1 for 10 mm plate
2. **Set pressures** — per tip manufacturer chart (see table above)
3. **Light torch and establish neutral preheat flame** — inner cone sharp, 1:1 fuel/oxygen ratio
4. **Position torch perpendicular (90°) to the plate** at the pierce location
5. **Begin preheat** — hold the torch with the inner cone tip 3–6 mm (1/8"–1/4") above the plate surface[^1]
6. **Wait for ignition temperature** — the steel will glow cherry-orange to bright orange (~870°C). This takes 10–30 seconds depending on thickness and ambient temperature.
7. **Tilt the torch ~75° from vertical** (15° from perpendicular) — this allows slag to blow away from the tip, not back into it[^1]
8. **Slowly open the cutting oxygen valve** — you'll see slag blow away through the plate if successful, or the flame may extinguish (too early — reheat and try again)
9. **Once a hole appears,** return the torch to 90° and begin cutting the desired shape

**Common pierce failures:**
- Slag back-blow onto the tip = didn't tilt the torch first
- Cut extinguishes = opened cutting oxygen too early (steel not hot enough)
- Hole larger than expected = tilted torch too much, or lingered too long after pierce

## Elliptical opening: layout and cutting

An elliptical opening (oval hole) is common in pressure vessel nozzle openings, ductwork transitions, and structural web penetrations.[^1]

### Layout

1. **Determine semi-major axis (a)** and **semi-minor axis (b)** from drawing
2. **Mark centre point** with a prick punch (a small dot only — not a deep punch mark that could initiate cracking)
3. **Method 1 — Trammel method:**
   - Cut a thin wooden or steel trammel bar
   - Mark two distances from one end: distance *b* and distance *a+b*
   - Pin one end to the centre. Pins at the two marks travel along perpendicular reference lines (X and Y axis)
   - Trace the ellipse
4. **Method 2 — Ellipse template** (for repetitive production work) — steel or polycarbonate template marked from the approved drawing, scribed around with a soapstone marker

### Cutting sequence

1. Pierce inside the marked line (waste material side) — allow 3 mm clearance inside the layout line so you can finish to size with a grinder
2. Cut the ellipse following the layout line — maintain consistent torch-to-plate distance (3–6 mm) and travel speed
3. For inside curves, angle the torch slightly toward the direction of travel to maintain the cut at the kerf face
4. Finish to line with a file or angle grinder; check against template

**The kerf eats into your material.** For a precise elliptical opening, start the pierce 3–6 mm inside the layout line. The kerf width for 10 mm plate at ~400 mm/min is approximately 1.5–3 mm.[^1]

## Cope cutting: fitting to structural shapes

A cope cut removes a notch from the end of a structural shape (beam, channel, angle) so it fits flush against another member.[^3]

### Procedure: cope 10 mm (3/8") mild steel C-shape to fit a 100 mm (4") C-channel

1. **Lay out the cope dimensions** from the drawing using a square and soapstone
2. **Mark the cope notch** — identify which flange material to remove and the exact inside radius at the root of the cope
3. **Drill or punch a relief hole** at the inside corner of the cope before cutting — this prevents the cut from continuing past the corner and notching the web (a stress concentration point). Minimum 6 mm diameter hole.[^1]
4. **Make the cope cuts** — cut the flange first (the part being removed), then the remaining web trim if needed
5. **Check fit against the mating member** — the cope should fit within the tolerance specified on the drawing, typically ±1.5 mm for structural work[^4]
6. **Grind the cope surface** smooth — cope edges become weld-prep surfaces; rough or oxidized surfaces prevent good fusion

### 30° bevel cut (weld preparation)

A 30° bevel (per-side) creates a 60° included groove angle, which is the most common V-groove prep for SMAW on plate.[^3]

**Technique:**
- Tilt the cutting torch 30° from vertical (60° from horizontal)
- Maintain the same tip-to-plate distance as for a square cut (the inclined tip means the inner cone is farther from the actual cut face — compensate by increasing preheat time slightly)
- Travel speed slightly slower than square cut (more material to heat through at the angle)
- Use a bevel gauge to set and verify the torch angle before cutting — clamp a bevel guide to the torch if available

**Critical:** The torch must maintain the same angle throughout the bevel cut. Wavering angle produces an uneven bevel face that requires grinding. Set up a guide rail if doing production bevel cutting.

## Numbers you need to memorize

- **Mild steel ignition temperature for oxyfuel cutting:** ~870°C (1600°F)[^3]
- **Preheat flame tip-to-plate distance:** 3–6 mm (1/8"–1/4")[^1]
- **Pierce tilt angle:** ~75° from vertical (15° from perpendicular to plate)[^1]
- **Kerf width on 10 mm plate (typical):** 1.5–3 mm[^1]
- **Cope relief hole minimum diameter:** 6 mm (1/4") — drilled before cutting[^1]
- **CSA W59 fit-up tolerance for structural cope:** typically ±1.5 mm[^4]
- **Common V-groove included angle for SMAW plate:** 60° (2 × 30° bevel per side)
- **Preheat time for 10 mm plate (typical):** 10–20 seconds at 3–6 mm tip distance[^1]

## What the textbook doesn't tell you

**The pierce tilt angle saves your tip.** This sounds minor but it's not — on a job where you're piercing 50 access holes per day, a clogged or burned tip costs time and money. The tilt lets the initial slag blast go away from the torch, not back into the ports. Make it habit, every pierce.

**Backing bars prevent slag drop-through on elevated work.** If you're cutting an access hole in a horizontal plate with nothing below it, the molten slag drops 10 m onto whatever's below. Scaffold decking, cables, yourself. On elevated work, clean the area below first, or put a steel catch pan. This is an OHS issue as well as a fire issue.[^2]

**Bevel angle varies by code and WPS.** The 30° bevel producing a 60° included groove is a standard, not a universal. Some WPS's specify 37.5° per side (75° included) for deep groove SMAW. Some structural codes allow 22.5° per side. Always cut to what the WPS says, not what you think is normal.

**Machine cutting (track burner) produces a far better bevel than hand cutting** for production work. Period 1 introduced the machine torch; Period 2 is where you start recognizing when to use it vs hand cutting. Structural fab shops will not accept hand-cut bevels on critical welds — the surface quality isn't good enough for radiographic inspection.

## Key terms

- **Pierce:** starting an oxyfuel cut in the interior of a plate (not from an edge)
- **Kerf:** the slot left by the cutting process; width depends on tip size and travel speed
- **Ignition temperature:** temperature at which the oxidation reaction becomes self-sustaining (~870°C for mild steel)
- **Cope:** a notch cut from the end of a structural member so it fits flush around another member
- **Bevel:** a chamfered edge cut at an angle to prepare a joint for welding
- **Elliptical opening:** an oval cut (axes not equal) common for vessel nozzles and penetrations
- **Trammel:** a layout tool with two adjustable pins used to scribe ellipses and arcs
- **Relief hole:** a drilled hole at the inside corner of a cope to prevent overcut
- **Backfire:** a brief retraction of the flame into the tip, producing a loud pop — caused by tip-to-work distance too small or touching the work
- **Flashback:** the flame burning inside the hose toward the regulator — a serious emergency requiring immediate valve closure and equipment inspection[^2]

## Common exam trap

- **Oxyfuel cutting does NOT work on stainless steel or cast iron** — the exam may describe a scenario and ask which material can be cut. Only mild and low-alloy steel cut cleanly by oxidation.
- **The pierce tilt prevents slag blow-back onto the tip** — not to improve penetration or not to prevent backfire. Be specific when answering "why do we tilt."
- **Kerf width is material removed from the cut** — the finished opening will be the layout line *minus* the kerf width if the cut follows the line exactly. Layout inside the line for finished openings.
- **Cope relief holes are drilled BEFORE cutting** — not punched, and not after. Punching can crack higher-carbon plate; drilling after means you've already made the overcut error.
- **Ignition temperature ≠ melting point.** Mild steel melts at ~1510°C; it ignites (begins oxidizing in oxygen) at ~870°C. Oxyfuel cutting happens far below the melting point — it's oxidation, not melting.

## Practice question preview

**Q:** While piercing a 10 mm mild steel plate for an access hole, the welder's cut extinguishes immediately after opening the cutting oxygen valve. What is the MOST likely cause?

A) The cutting oxygen pressure is too high
B) The tip is too large for the plate thickness
C) The steel had not reached ignition temperature before the cutting oxygen was opened
D) The torch was tilted too far from vertical

**Correct: C**

**Explanation:** The self-sustaining oxidation reaction requires the steel to be at its ignition temperature (~870°C) before cutting oxygen is introduced. If the oxygen is opened too soon, it cools the steel below ignition temperature and the cut extinguishes. (A) High cutting oxygen pressure would cause a rough or wide kerf, not extinction. (B) A tip that is too large wastes oxygen but won't cause extinction — the preheat is simply oversized. (D) Tilting too far might cause slag issues but won't extinguish the cut once ignition temperature is reached.
