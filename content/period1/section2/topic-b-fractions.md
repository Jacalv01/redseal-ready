# Fractions

Every dimension on a welding drawing that uses imperial measurement is a fraction. Electrode diameter, plate thickness, weld size, pipe schedule, root opening — all of it in fractions. If you can't quickly add 3/8 + 1/4, you can't lay out a joint correctly. This isn't school math — it's the difference between a part that fits and a part that gets scrapped.

---

## Fraction vocabulary — know these cold

| Term | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Numerator** | The top number — how many parts you have | **3**/4 — you have 3 parts |
| **Denominator** | The bottom number — how many equal parts in the whole | 3/**4** — the whole is divided into 4 equal parts |
| **Proper fraction** | Numerator is less than denominator (value < 1) | 3/4, 7/16, 5/8 |
| **Improper fraction** | Numerator is greater than or equal to denominator (value ≥ 1) | 9/4, 16/16 |
| **Mixed number** | A whole number plus a proper fraction | 2 3/4, 1 7/8 |
| **Equivalent fractions** | Different fractions that represent the same value | 1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8 = 8/16 |
| **Lowest terms / simplest form** | Fraction where numerator and denominator share no common factor > 1 | 4/8 simplified = 1/2 |
| **Common denominator** | A denominator shared by two or more fractions — required for adding/subtracting | Adding 1/4 + 3/8: common denominator = 8 |
| **Least common denominator (LCD)** | The smallest common denominator | LCD of 4 and 8 = 8 |

---

## Reading a tape measure — practical fractions for welders

Imperial tape measures divide each inch into **16 equal parts** (sixteenths) — the smallest common division on a standard tape.[^1]

**The hierarchy:**

```
1 inch
├─ 1/2 inch (8/16)
│   ├─ 1/4 inch (4/16)   and   3/4 inch (12/16)
│   │   ├─ 1/8 inch (2/16)     3/8 (6/16)     5/8 (10/16)     7/8 (14/16)
│   │   │   ├─ 1/16 inch
│   │   │   ├─ 3/16 inch
│   │   │   ├─ 5/16 inch
│   │   │   ├─ 7/16 inch
│   │   │   ├─ 9/16 inch
│   │   │   ├─ 11/16 inch
│   │   │   ├─ 13/16 inch
│   │   │   └─ 15/16 inch
```

**Tape mark identification:**[^1]

- **Longest marks** = full inches
- **Second longest** = 1/2 inch
- **Third longest** = 1/4 and 3/4 inch marks
- **Medium marks** = eighths (1/8, 3/8, 5/8, 7/8)
- **Short marks** = sixteenths

**Common welding dimensions in fractions:**

| Decimal equivalent | Fraction | What it is in welding |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0625 in | 1/16 in | Minimum root opening for thin plate SMAW |
| 0.125 in | 1/8 in | Common SMAW electrode diameter (E7018) |
| 0.1875 in | 3/16 in | Small fillet weld size; thin plate root face |
| 0.25 in | 1/4 in | Common fillet weld minimum; plate thickness |
| 0.3125 in | 5/16 in | Fillet weld size for 3/8 in plate (minimum per CSA W59 for fillet on this thickness) |
| 0.375 in | 3/8 in | Very common plate thickness in fabrication |
| 0.5 in | 1/2 in | Common plate thickness; root opening for pipe groove |
| 0.75 in | 3/4 in | Heavier plate thickness |
| 1.0 in | 1 in | Full inch — base for mixed number reading |

## Simplifying fractions

Before you can add or subtract fractions, you often need a common denominator. Before you can compare them, it helps to simplify.[^2]

**Method: divide numerator and denominator by their Greatest Common Factor (GCF)**

**Example: Simplify 12/16**

Step 1: Find the GCF of 12 and 16.
- Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
- Factors of 16: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16
- GCF = 4

Step 2: Divide both by the GCF:
- 12 ÷ 4 = 3
- 16 ÷ 4 = 4
- Result: **3/4**

**Welding example:** You measure a gap of 12/16 inches. Simplified: 3/4 inches. You record 3/4" on your inspection sheet.

---

## Adding fractions — step by step

**Rule:** You can only add fractions with the same denominator. Get a common denominator first.[^2]

### Same denominator (easy case)
Add the numerators; keep the denominator.

**Example:** 3/8 + 1/8 = **4/8 = 1/2**

### Different denominators (the real case)
Find the LCD, convert both fractions, then add.

**Welding example:** A weld bead on the first pass measures 3/16 inch high. After the second pass it measures 5/8 inch high. What is the total height added by both passes?

Wait — you can't add 3/16 to 5/8 directly; let's set this up correctly: you're measuring the second pass height as additional height above the first: 5/8 - 3/16.

Let's do an addition example instead:

**Example:** Two plates are tacked together. One plate is 3/8 inch thick. The other is 1/4 inch thick. What is the total combined thickness?

Step 1: LCD of 8 and 4 = 8

Step 2: Convert 1/4 to eighths: 1/4 × 2/2 = 2/8

Step 3: Add: 3/8 + 2/8 = **5/8 inch**

**Answer: The combined thickness is 5/8 inch.**

---

## Subtracting fractions

Same rule: get a common denominator, then subtract numerators.

**Welding example:** The drawing calls for a 5/8 inch root face. Your saw cut left a root face of 7/16 inch. How much more do you need to grind off? Wait — you're already under, so you've over-ground. Let's reframe:

**Example:** You need a 3/4 inch root opening but the current gap measures 5/16 inch. How much do you need to open up the gap?

Step 1: LCD of 4 and 16 = 16

Step 2: Convert 3/4 to sixteenths: 3/4 × 4/4 = 12/16

Step 3: Subtract: 12/16 − 5/16 = **7/16 inch**

**Answer: You need to increase the gap by 7/16 inch.**

---

## Multiplying fractions

Multiply numerator × numerator and denominator × denominator. Then simplify.[^2]

**Example:** You need to cut 6 pieces of flat bar, each 3/4 inch wide, from a larger piece. How wide a strip do you need in total?

6 × 3/4 = (6 × 3)/(1 × 4) = 18/4 = **4 1/2 inches** (improper fraction → mixed number: 18 ÷ 4 = 4 remainder 2, so 4 and 2/4 = 4 1/2 inches)

---

## Dividing fractions

Invert the second fraction (divisor) and multiply.[^2]

**"Keep, Change, Flip":** Keep the first fraction, Change ÷ to ×, Flip the second fraction.

**Welding example:** You have a 7 1/2 inch length of flat bar to divide into equal pieces, each 3/8 inch wide (say for spacer shims). How many pieces can you cut?

Step 1: Convert 7 1/2 to an improper fraction: (7 × 2 + 1)/2 = 15/2

Step 2: Divide: 15/2 ÷ 3/8 = 15/2 × 8/3 = 120/6 = **20 pieces**

**Answer: You can cut 20 shim pieces.**

---

## Converting between mixed numbers and improper fractions

**Mixed → Improper:** Multiply the whole number by the denominator, add the numerator.

**Example:** 2 3/4 → (2 × 4 + 3)/4 = 11/4

**Improper → Mixed:** Divide numerator by denominator; remainder becomes the new numerator.

**Example:** 11/4 → 11 ÷ 4 = 2 remainder 3 → **2 3/4**

**Why welders care:** A pipe end prep is specified at 1 7/16 inch from the weld bevel to a reference mark. Your calculations give you 23/16 inch. Are these the same? Convert: 23 ÷ 16 = 1 remainder 7 → **1 7/16 inch. Yes, they're the same.**[^1]

## Applied welding calculation — full worked example

**Scenario:** You're laying out a weld joint for a built-up H-beam. The web plate is 3/8 inch thick. You need to position a fillet weld on both sides of the web-to-flange joint. The drawing calls for a 1/4 inch fillet weld on each side. You need to find the total weld metal "reach" (both fillet welds combined).

Step 1: Each fillet weld leg is 1/4 inch. Two welds = 2 × 1/4 = 2/4 = **1/2 inch total fillet weld width** across both sides.

Step 2: Web thickness = 3/8 inch. The flanges extend 1/4 inch beyond each side of the web = 2 × 1/4 = 1/2 inch total flange extension beyond the web.

Step 3: Total flange width used by web + fillets = 3/8 + 2(1/4) = 3/8 + 2/4.

LCD of 8 and 4 = 8. Convert 2/4 = 4/8.

3/8 + 4/8 = **7/8 inch** of flange width consumed by the joint.

## Numbers you need to memorize

- **Tape measure: 16 divisions per inch** — the smallest mark on a standard imperial tape is 1/16 in[^1]
- **Order of mark lengths on tape:** longest = full inch; then 1/2; then 1/4; then 1/8; shortest = 1/16[^1]
- **Common electrode diameters:** 3/32", 1/8", 5/32", 3/16" — all fractions[^1]
- **Common plate thicknesses:** 3/16", 1/4", 5/16", 3/8", 1/2", 5/8", 3/4", 1"[^1]
- **Adding fractions requires a common denominator** — you cannot add 1/4 + 3/8 as-is[^2]
- **Dividing fractions: Keep, Change, Flip** — invert the divisor and multiply[^2]

---

## What the textbook doesn't tell you

**A tape measure that reads to 1/16 inch is not fine enough for precision fit-up.** In structural fab the tape is fine. In pressure vessel or precision machined fit-up work, you need a rule graduated to 1/32 inch (or calipers). Know which tool is appropriate for the precision required.[^1]

**The "reduce before you multiply" trick saves a ton of arithmetic.** When multiplying two fractions, if any numerator and any denominator share a common factor, cancel them before multiplying. Example: 4/9 × 3/8 — the 4 and 8 share a factor of 4 (simplify to 1/2), the 3 and 9 share a factor of 3 (simplify to 1/3): result = 1/2 × 1/3 = 1/6. Much faster than multiplying first to get 12/72 = 1/6.[^2]

**Fractions are still used for pipe and fitting specifications in Canada.** Nominal pipe size (NPS) and ANSI/ASME fittings are specified in inches and fractions. Even shops that work in metric for plate work will use fractional inches for pipe components. Don't assume "metric shop = no fractions."[^1]

## Key terms

- **Numerator:** the top number in a fraction — how many parts you have
- **Denominator:** the bottom number — how many equal parts make the whole
- **Mixed number:** whole number + proper fraction (e.g., 2 3/4)
- **Improper fraction:** numerator ≥ denominator (e.g., 11/4)
- **Equivalent fractions:** same value expressed with different numerator/denominator
- **Simplest form / lowest terms:** fraction reduced so numerator and denominator share no factor > 1
- **Least common denominator (LCD):** smallest denominator that both fractions can be converted to — required for addition/subtraction
- **GCF (Greatest Common Factor):** largest factor shared by numerator and denominator — used for simplification

---

## Common exam trap

- **"You can add 1/4 + 3/8 by adding 1+3 and 4+8"** — this gives 4/12 = 1/3, which is WRONG. Adding fractions without a common denominator is the most common error. The correct answer is 2/8 + 3/8 = 5/8.
- **"5/4 is not a valid fraction"** — wrong. Improper fractions are valid mathematical expressions. 5/4 = 1 1/4. Mixed numbers and improper fractions are interchangeable.
- **Reading a tape measure:** the second-longest marks are halves, not quarters. Incorrectly calling the quarter-inch marks "halves" and misreading everything by a factor of two is a real measurement error that causes real scrap.
- **"Multiply fractions the same way you add them — get a common denominator"** — false. Multiplication does NOT require a common denominator. Keep, multiply numerators, multiply denominators, simplify. Adding is what requires the common denominator.

## Practice question preview

**Q:** A welder needs to cut a plate to a length of 4 3/8 inches from a piece that is 12 1/4 inches long. How much plate remains after the cut (ignoring saw kerf)?

A) 8 1/8 inches  
B) 7 7/8 inches  
C) 8 3/8 inches  
D) 7 1/8 inches  

**Correct: B**

**Explanation:**
- Convert to improper fractions (or find LCD and subtract): 12 1/4 − 4 3/8
- LCD of 4 and 8 = 8
- 12 1/4 = 12 2/8
- 12 2/8 − 4 3/8: borrow 1 from 12 → 11 10/8 − 4 3/8 = **7 7/8 inches**
- Option A (8 1/8) is wrong — sign error. Option C (8 3/8) adds instead of subtracts. Option D (7 1/8) makes an error in the numerator subtraction.
