# Power Tools

The angle grinder is the most dangerous tool in a welding shop by a large margin. Not the arc, not the cylinders — the grinder. A burst grinding disc can penetrate a steel toe boot, sever a femoral artery, or embed fragments in a face. This lesson is partly about what the tools do, and mostly about not becoming the incident report.

---

## Grinders

### Types of grinders used by welders

| Type | Wheel type | Primary use |
|---|---|---|
| **Angle grinder (side grinder)** | Depressed-centre grinding disc; cutting discs; flap discs; wire cup | Most versatile — weld grinding, cutting, deburring |
| **Bench grinder (pedestal grinder)** | Straight grinding wheel | Tool dressing, sharpening electrodes, edge prep |
| **Die grinder (pencil grinder)** | Mounted points, carbide burrs | Access tight areas, internal root grinding |
| **Belt sander** | Abrasive belt | Surface finishing, edge deburring |

### Angle grinder — detailed operation and safety

The angle grinder is the dominant grinding tool for welders. Operating it safely requires discipline that journeypersons reinforce constantly.[^4]

**Before you start — inspect the disc/wheel:**

1. **Ring test:** tap the disc with a pencil or knuckle. A good disc rings with a clear, resonant tone. A cracked disc produces a dull thud. Cracked disc = throw it in the bin.[^1]
2. **Visual inspection:** look for chips, cracks, delamination, or centre hole damage.
3. **Rated RPM:** every abrasive disc has a **maximum RPM stamped or printed on it**. Match the disc's maximum RPM to the grinder's no-load RPM. Never mount a disc rated lower than the grinder's speed — the disc will fail catastrophically. A 4.5 in. grinder at 11 000 RPM requires a disc rated for at least 11 000 RPM.[^1] [^4]
4. **Disc diameter compatibility:** match disc diameter to the grinder. A larger disc on a smaller grinder overloads the arbor and guard clearance.
5. **Guard:** the grinding guard must be in place and positioned to direct debris away from the operator. Do not remove or bypass guards — this is both an OHS Code violation and the reason grinding disc injuries often involve facial injuries.[^1]

**PPE for grinding:**
- Face shield (full face) **over** safety glasses — eye protection alone is not enough for grinding; a flying disc fragment can enter below safety glass frames
- Heavy leather gloves
- FR clothing — grinding sparks ignite dust, cloth, and flammable liquids
- Hearing protection — angle grinding is 95–105 dBA, well above the 85 dBA action level[^1]

**Operating an angle grinder:**

1. Ensure the grinder is **off and unplugged** before mounting or changing any disc.
2. Mount the disc with the correct flange orientation — the reinforced (recessed) side of a depressed-centre disc faces the grinder body, not outward.
3. Tighten the arbor nut with the pin spanner — **hand tight is never enough**; the grinding force will spin the disc on the arbor.
4. Reinstall the guard.
5. Plug in / power on. Allow the grinder to reach full speed (2–3 seconds) **before** touching the work.
6. Apply moderate pressure — excessive pressure overloads the disc, causes deflection, and accelerates disc failure. Let the abrasive do the work.
7. **Keep the disc at the correct angle:** flat discs at about **15–30°** to the workpiece surface. Grinding disc is not for cutting — a cutting disc is for cutting; using a grinding disc to make cuts or scoring causes lateral loading, disc flexion, and failure.
8. **Cutting discs are for cutting, not grinding.** A thin cutting disc (1–2 mm) will shatter if used for side grinding. Use it only in the plane of the disc cut — straight through, no rocking.
9. **Stand clear of the disc plane** when starting up and during cuts — if the disc bursts, fragments travel in the plane of the disc.
10. Allow the grinder to come to a complete stop before setting it down.

### Bench grinder

The bench grinder has two wheels (typically: one coarse for fast material removal, one fine for finishing). Used by welders mainly for:[^2]

- Dressing grinding wheel faces (truing with a wheel dresser)
- Sharpening tungsten electrodes (for GTAW — more on this in Period 2)
- Removing hardened scale from tool edges

**Bench grinder safety:**
- **Tool rest:** keep the tool rest adjusted to within **3 mm (1/8 in.)** of the wheel face. A larger gap lets material wedge between the rest and wheel, potentially shattering the wheel or jamming the workpiece.[^1]
- **Eye shield:** the bench grinder eye shield is a minimum — add safety glasses. Wheel particles spray upward and sideways from the contact zone.
- Never grind on the side of a straight wheel — side loading can fracture it.
- **Stand to the side** when starting a bench grinder — if the wheel shatters on startup (cold wheel, previous crack), it throws fragments forward and to the side.

## Drills

### Types

- **Corded drill / cordless drill:** variable-speed trigger, keyless or keyed chuck. For welders, primarily used for drilling holes in plate and structural steel before or after welding.
- **Magnetic drill (mag drill):** an electromagnetic base that clamps the drill securely to a steel surface, keeping the drill perpendicular to the work surface for accurate holes without a drill press or fixture. Used extensively in structural work for bolt holes.[^2]
- **Drill press (pedestal drill):** fixed machine drill — most accurate for repeated hole drilling in a shop environment.

### Drill bit types

- **Twist drill (high-speed steel — HSS):** standard for most steel. Available in mm or fractional inch sizes.
- **Step drill (Unibit):** for thin sheet and thin-wall tube — creates clean holes without the deburring that twist drills leave in thin material.
- **Annular cutter (hole saw for steel):** for larger diameter holes — removes a circular core rather than drilling solid; much less force required than a twist drill for large diameters. Requires a mag drill or drill press.

### Drilling safety

- **Secure the workpiece** — a drill that catches in a hole spins the workpiece with violent force if the drill is not free to rotate with it (vice, clamp, or fixture required — never hold by hand for anything above 6 mm holes).
- **Cutting fluid (cutting oil):** use for any drilling in steel over 6 mm — extends drill bit life, reduces heat, and produces cleaner holes.
- **Correct RPM for drill diameter and material:** larger drills run slower; softer materials run faster. For HSS drills in mild steel, typical surface speed is 25–30 m/min — this calculates to different RPM for different drill diameters.[^2]
- Never wear gloves while drilling on a drill press — if the bit catches, a glove can wrap around the chuck and drag your hand.

## Metal forming and shaping tools

### Hydraulic press / shop press

Used for pressing apart and together components, straightening bent plate, and pressing bearings. Capacity typically 10–100 tonne in shop environments. Always use appropriate dies — press blocks, V-blocks, or flat plates to distribute force without indenting or marking the work.

### Sheet metal brake (press brake)

Bends sheet and plate to specified angles. In welding shops, typically a hydraulic press brake. The punch (upper die) presses the material into the V-shaped lower die. Bend angle and spring-back are calculated from material thickness and tensile strength — mild steel springs back slightly less than high-strength steel.[^2]

### Roll bender (slip roll)

Rolls flat plate into cylindrical or curved sections. Three-roll designs are standard. Used in pressure vessel fabrication and duct work. The plate feeds between the rolls and the top roll is pressed down to control the bend radius.

### Tube and pipe bender

Mechanical or hydraulic benders that form pipe/tube to specific bend radii without collapsing the wall. Match the bender tooling (mandrel, former) to the pipe OD and wall thickness — undersized tooling causes ovality and wall collapse.

## Metal cutting tools (power)

### Reciprocating saw (Sawzall)

For rough cuts in structural steel when an angle grinder or oxyfuel torch is impractical. Bi-metal blades for steel. Not for precise cuts — the blade flexes. Used for demolition cuts, pipe cuts in constrained spaces.[^2]

### Cold saw (circular cold saw)

A circular saw with a tipped or solid HSS blade running at low RPM. Produces a precise, burr-free cut with a narrow kerf. Unlike angle grinding, the saw produces chips, not sparks — used in shops where sparks are a concern (adjacent to spray booths, etc.). Blade speed: 20–80 RPM depending on material and blade diameter.

### Band saw (metal cutting band saw)

A continuous loop blade for precision straight or curved cuts. Used for cutting bar stock, structural shapes, pipe, and tube in shop environments. Blade selection (TPI, material) follows the same 3-teeth-in-contact rule as hacksaws.[^2]

### Plasma cutter (for context — covered fully in Topic J)

Power-tool plasma arc cutting is included here as a power metal cutting tool — it uses electrical power and compressed air (or inert gas) to create a plasma arc that cuts any conductive metal. Topic J covers it in detail.

## Power positioners

A **welding positioner** is a powered fixture that rotates and/or tilts the workpiece to bring the weld joint into the optimal welding position — typically flat (1G/1F) — rather than requiring the welder to work overhead or vertical.[^3]

### Benefits

- Improved weld quality (flat position welds are easier to execute and typically higher quality than overhead)
- Reduced welder fatigue
- Increased productivity (flat position welding allows higher deposition rates)
- Consistent, repeatable positioning for production welding

### Types of positioners

| Type | Function | Typical application |
|---|---|---|
| **Headstock/tailstock positioner** | Two-ended support, rotates the work along a horizontal axis | Long pipes, shafts, vessels |
| **Tilt-and-turn table (turntable positioner)** | Tilts and rotates — 2-axis movement | General fabrication, flange welding |
| **Manipulator** | Fixed or movable boom with the torch attached — the torch moves, not the work | Large vessel circumferential welding (SAW or GMAW) |
| **Fit-up positioner (fit-up stand)** | Supports large assemblies at a fixed height for fit-up before transfer to a welding positioner | Tacking structural assemblies |

### Operating a positioner

- **Check rated capacity** — positioners have both a weight limit (total load) and a moment capacity (offset load × distance from table centre). A 500 kg positioner with a workpiece where the CG is 500 mm from the table centreline may exceed the moment limit even if the weight is within the rated limit.[^3]
- **Fix the workpiece securely** — typically bolted through the table face or clamped in a fixture. A workpiece that shifts on the positioner table during welding is a severe hazard.
- **Rotate speed control:** welding on a rotating positioner requires matching travel speed to the positioner RPM — the combination determines the heat input per unit length.

## Numbers you need to memorize

- **Grinder disc RPM:** must be **equal to or greater than** the grinder's no-load RPM — never less[^1]
- **Ring test:** clear resonant tone = intact disc; dull thud = cracked — remove from service[^1]
- **Bench grinder tool rest gap:** maximum **3 mm (1/8 in.)** from the wheel face[^1]
- **Noise level during grinding:** **95–105 dBA** — hearing protection required[^1]
- **Cutting disc vs grinding disc:** cutting disc (1–2 mm thin) for cuts only; grinding disc (5–6 mm thick) for grinding only — cannot substitute one for the other[^4]
- **Angle to workpiece for grinding:** approximately **15–30°** for a grinding disc[^4]
- **Stand to the side** of a grinder disc plane on startup — fragments travel in the disc plane[^4]

## What the textbook doesn't tell you

**The angle grinder guard is a life safety device, not an inconvenience.** It's repositioned constantly — but it is never removed. The guard channels disc fragments downward if a disc shatters. Without the guard, fragments travel in a full circle, and at 11 000 RPM, a 4.5-inch disc fragment hits with energy comparable to a rifle round. The only reason "remove the guard" seems like a shortcut is because disc failures are rare — until they're not.[^4]

**Disc RPM is easy to match — but people don't check.** The rated RPM is printed on every disc. The grinder's no-load RPM is on its nameplate. Two seconds of checking. Apprentices who work in shops with multiple grinder sizes and one shared container of discs get this wrong regularly — a disc rated for a 4.5-inch tool at 11 000 RPM mounted on a 5-inch tool running at 9 000 RPM is fine. The reverse (slow-rated disc on a fast grinder) is a burst waiting to happen.[^1]

**Mag drills create hands-free drilling — but the workpiece must be clean.** A mag drill's electromagnetic hold-down is only as good as the contact between the base and the steel. Rust, scale, or a piece of weld spatter under one corner of the base reduces holding force to nearly nothing. Clean the base area before every setup; test the hold by pushing before drilling.[^2]

**Positioner rated capacity is a two-number check.** Everyone checks the weight limit. Almost nobody checks the moment capacity when they have an asymmetric workpiece. A 1,000 kg positioner with 1,000 kg of plate hanging 800 mm off-centre can exceed the moment rating — the table tilts, the work crashes. Check both numbers, every time.[^3]

## Key terms

- **RPM (revolutions per minute):** rotational speed of a grinding disc, drill bit, or saw blade — must be matched to the tool's rated speed and the disc's rated speed
- **Ring test:** impact test for abrasive discs — checks for cracks by listening for a resonant ring vs. dull thud
- **Arbor:** the central threaded shaft of a grinder on which the disc is mounted
- **Guard:** the protective hood or shroud around a grinder disc — required at all times during operation
- **Moment (torque load):** the product of load mass × distance from the axis of rotation — determines positioner tipping risk
- **Magnetic drill (mag drill):** a drill with an electromagnetic base that clamps to a steel surface for field drilling
- **Positioner:** a powered work-holding fixture that rotates and/or tilts the weld joint into the optimal position
- **Cold saw:** a low-RPM circular saw with a tipped blade — cuts without generating significant heat (hence "cold")
- **Annular cutter:** a core-drilling tool that removes a cylindrical slug to create large-diameter holes with minimal force

## Common exam trap

- **Ring test (cracked disc):** the result of a cracked disc in the ring test is a **dull thud**, not a high-pitched ring. Distractors reverse this — "a cracked disc rings clearly." It doesn't.
- **Cutting disc used for grinding:** wrong — not because of performance, but because a cutting disc (thin, brittle) shatters under the lateral (grinding) load. The distractor claims "a cutting disc can be used for light grinding." It cannot.
- **Guard removal for better access:** always incorrect. No OHS or safety code provision allows removing a grinder guard for easier access. The correct answer is always "reposition the guard" or "use a different tool."
- **Bench grinder tool rest gap:** the 3 mm (1/8 in.) maximum gap. Distractors may offer 6 mm, 10 mm, or "adjust to user preference."
- **Positioner capacity — moment vs weight:** the question may describe a workpiece that is within weight capacity but off-centre. The correct reasoning is to also check moment capacity. Answering "within weight limit, proceed" is wrong.
- **Drill press vs mag drill for accuracy:** the drill press is more accurate (fixed perpendicularity). The mag drill is used when the workpiece cannot be brought to the drill press (field work, large structural members). Distractors may reverse this.

## Practice question preview

**Q:** An angle grinder is rated at 11 000 RPM (no-load). A 115 mm (4.5 in.) grinding disc labelled "Maximum 10 200 RPM" is available. What should the welder do?

A) Use the disc — 800 RPM difference is within the 10% tolerance for abrasive products  
B) Use the disc only for light grinding (half trigger pressure reduces effective RPM below the limit)  
C) Do not use the disc — it must not be used on this grinder because its maximum rated RPM is less than the grinder's no-load RPM  
D) Use the disc only for cutting (cutting produces lower RPM than grinding)  

**Correct: C**

**Explanation:** An abrasive disc must have a maximum RPM rating **equal to or greater than** the grinder's no-load RPM. At 11 000 RPM (grinder) vs 10 200 RPM maximum (disc), the disc is under-rated for this machine. The no-load RPM is the speed the grinder reaches before it touches any load — so the disc will be spun to 11 000 RPM immediately on startup, exceeding its rated burst speed. There is no "10% tolerance" in Canadian or ANSI abrasive wheel standards. Trigger pressure control does not reliably limit grinder RPM to safe levels. The disc must be discarded or used only on a grinder rated at or below 10 200 RPM no-load.
