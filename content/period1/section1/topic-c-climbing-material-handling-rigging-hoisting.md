# Climbing and Material Handling: Lifting, Rigging and Hoisting

Drop a rigged load in a shop and someone dies. Period. Rigging is the skill that separates welders who stay employed from welders who get someone killed.

---

## Climbing safety

Every elevated work platform a welder uses — ladders, scaffolding, man baskets, aerial work platforms — has specific rules that can and will be enforced by site safety officers.

### Ladders

Alberta OHS Code Part 23 sets the rules.[^1] The ones that come up in incidents every year:

- **3-point contact at all times** — two feet + one hand OR two hands + one foot. Never both hands carrying materials while climbing.
- **Extension ladders:** set at a **4:1 ratio** (1 foot out from the wall for every 4 feet of height). A 12-foot wall means the base sits 3 feet out.
- Ladders must extend at least **1 m (3 ft) above the landing point** they access.
- **Never use the top cap or top rung** of a stepladder as a standing surface.
- Defective ladders (bent rungs, cracked rails, broken feet) are tagged and removed from service — not leaned against the wall for "light work."

### Scaffolding and aerial work platforms

- Falls from scaffolding are the number-one killer in construction trades. On any scaffold over **3 m (10 ft)**, guardrails and toeboards are mandatory.[^1]
- Aerial work platforms (boom lifts, scissor lifts) require OEM-specific operator training. Boom lifts add fall arrest requirements even inside the basket — one anchor, full harness, not just a lanyard.
- Always check load capacity of the platform **before** stacking material on it. Welders who park rolls of wire, electrode buckets, and a grinder on a scissor lift platform are exceeding rated capacity.

## Material handling: safe lifting

Manual lifting is a leading cause of musculoskeletal injury in the trades. Before you touch a load:

1. **Size up the load** — weight, balance, grip points. If you can't see clearly, ask someone.
2. **Clear your path** — tripping hazards, swing radius, overhead obstacles.
3. **Use your legs, not your back** — stand close, feet shoulder-width, bend your knees, keep your back in a neutral curve. Lift by straightening your legs, not curling your spine.
4. **Team lifts** — anything over **23 kg (50 lb)** is generally a two-person lift, though actual limits depend on frequency, posture, and distance travelled. When in doubt, get help or use a mechanical aid.[^4]
5. **Never twist while lifting** — if you need to turn, move your feet.

### Handling and storage of materials

- Steel plate and structural shapes stored vertically must be **racked** or blocked against tipping — do not lean against a single wall without restraint.
- Cylinders (oxygen, acetylene, propane, argon, CO₂): chained to a cart or wall bracket, **valve caps on** when not connected, stored upright.
- Long sections (pipe, angle, channel) stored horizontally need support at multiple points — sagging from single-end support creates dangerous spring-back energy when cut free.
- Segregate hazardous materials from ignition sources per WHMIS/SDS and CSA W117.2 requirements.[^4]

## Weight and centre of gravity

You cannot rig safely unless you know what the load weighs and where it balances. Getting this wrong means the load rolls, tips, or the sling angle blows past its safe limit.

### Calculating load weight

For common shapes, use these formulas:[^4]

**Mild steel plate:**
`Weight (kg) = Length (m) × Width (m) × Thickness (m) × 7 850`
- Steel density = **7 850 kg/m³** (7.85 g/cm³)[^4]
- Example: A plate 2 m × 1 m × 0.025 m (1 inch) = 2 × 1 × 0.025 × 7 850 = **392 kg**

**Solid round bar:**
`Weight (kg) = π/4 × Diameter² (m) × Length (m) × 7 850`

**Pipe:** weight per metre is stamped in manufacturer data tables — always use the published figure, not an estimate.

For imperial units (lb): `lb = L (ft) × W (ft) × T (in) × 40.8` for steel plate. This approximation (40.8 lb per sq ft per inch of thickness) is widely used on the job.[^4]

### Centre of gravity

For uniform shapes (plate, bar, beam), the centre of gravity (CG) is at the geometric centre — easy. For fabricated assemblies with flanges, cutouts, or attachments, CG shifts toward the heavier end.

**Trial lift rule:** Test rig any non-symmetric load at a fraction of full height (a few inches off the deck). If the load tilts, the CG is on the low side of the tilt — adjust your pick points before full lift.

Two-leg bridle sling picks:
- Equal-length legs from a centered pick point → equal load sharing, load lifts level.
- If load hangs off-centre, the short leg carries more load — you must re-rig or use a shackle shift to rebalance.

## Wire rope and synthetic sling capacity

### Sling angle matters — a lot

The **sling angle** is the angle between the sling leg and the horizontal plane of the load. The steeper the sling (closer to vertical), the more efficiently it lifts. A shallow angle (nearly horizontal) multiplies the tension in each sling leg dramatically.[^2]

| Included angle (°) | Sling leg angle from horizontal | Load factor per leg |
|---|---|---|
| 60° included | 60° from horizontal | 1.00 (full WLL applies) |
| 90° included | 45° from horizontal | 0.71 × WLL per leg |
| 120° included | 30° from horizontal | 0.50 × WLL per leg |

**Rule of thumb:** Never rig a 2-leg bridle at an included angle greater than **120°**. Below 30° leg angle from horizontal, tension in the legs can exceed the working load limit of the sling itself.

### Wire rope (IWRC / CFWRC)

Wire rope capacity is set by the manufacturer and varies by:
- **Construction** (6×19, 6×37, strand core vs independent wire rope core)
- **Grade** (IPS, EIPS, EEIPS — each grade is stronger than the last)
- **Diameter**

Working Load Limits (WLL) for a vertical hitch using bright wire rope IPS/IWRC (common in shops):[^2]

| Rope Diameter | Approx WLL — Vertical hitch |
|---|---|
| 6 mm (1/4 in) | ~1 100 kg (~2,400 lb) |
| 10 mm (3/8 in) | ~2 400 kg (~5,300 lb) |
| 13 mm (1/2 in) | ~4 400 kg (~9,800 lb) |
| 19 mm (3/4 in) | ~9 800 kg (~21,400 lb) |

**Always check the manufacturer data tag on the sling itself** — these are published approximations. The tag and documentation for the specific sling controls.

**Hitch configurations change WLL:**
- **Vertical hitch** = 1× rated load
- **Choker hitch** = ~75% of rated load (the choke angle reduces efficiency)
- **Basket hitch (balanced)** = 2× rated load

### Synthetic slings (web and round sling)

Nylon and polyester flat web slings are rated by color code:[^2]

| Color | WLL (vertical hitch) |
|---|---|
| Purple | 1 tonne (2,200 lb) |
| Green | 2 tonnes (4,400 lb) |
| Yellow | 3 tonnes (6,600 lb) |
| Tan/beige | 4 tonnes (8,800 lb) |
| Red | 5 tonnes (11,000 lb) |
| White | 6 tonnes (13,200 lb) |
| Blue | 8 tonnes (17,600 lb) |
| Orange | 10 tonnes (22,000 lb) |

**Critical: synthetic slings are not heat resistant.** Never use them near welding operations, sparks, or hot metal surfaces — they melt silently and without warning. Wire rope or chain only around welding heat.

**Inspect every sling before use:**
- Wire rope: broken wires (reject if 10% of wires in any strand are broken over one lay length)[^2]
- Synthetic: cuts, abrasion, UV degradation, heat damage, chemcial damage — any visible damage = remove from service
- Tags: must be legible and present. No tag = no use.

## Plate clamps and cable clips

### Plate clamps

Plate clamps (also called lifting clamps or beam clamps) are rated locking devices that grip steel plate by jaw pressure. They come in **horizontal** (for flat lifts) and **vertical** (for edge lifts or transport) variants.

**Rules:**[^3]
- Match the clamp to the plate thickness range marked on the clamp itself — using a clamp on plate thinner than its minimum rated thickness means the jaw cannot grip properly.
- Never use a horizontal plate clamp for a vertical lift or vice versa.
- Open clamp jaws fully before fitting — forcing a closed jaw onto a plate edge deforms the clamping mechanism.
- Check the WLL tag on the clamp. Multiple clamps used together: each clamp carries its share of the load, but sling geometry still applies.

### Cable/wire rope clips (bulldog clips)

Cable clips are used to form an eye (loop) in the end of wire rope — creating a field-termination sling or anchor point. Getting them wrong is a silent killer — the clip looks solid until it pulls apart under load.

**Correct installation (the "saddle to the dead end" rule):**[^2]
- The **U-bolt goes on the dead (short) end** of the rope.
- The **saddle (forged body) bears on the live (load-bearing) end**.
- **Never saddle the dead horse** — the phrase used on site to remind you not to reverse the clip.
- Spacing between clips: minimum **6 × rope diameter** (e.g., 13 mm rope → 78 mm clip spacing).
- Minimum clips for eye formation:
  - 6 mm rope: 2 clips
  - 10 mm rope: 3 clips
  - 13 mm–19 mm rope: 4 clips[^2]
- **Re-torque after the first load is applied.** Wire rope compresses slightly under initial loading and the clip nuts loosen. First lift → release load → retorque → proceed.

## Numbers you need to memorize

- **Steel density:** 7 850 kg/m³ (7.85 g/cm³) — used for all load weight calculations[^4]
- **Two-person lift guideline:** loads exceeding 23 kg (50 lb)[^4]
- **Maximum included sling angle:** 120° — at 120°, each sling leg carries 50% of rated WLL; at 90°, it's 71%[^2]
- **Choker hitch WLL reduction:** 75% of rated capacity[^2]
- **Basket hitch WLL boost:** 200% of rated capacity[^2]
- **Scaffold guardrail trigger:** > 3 m (10 ft) above lower level[^1]
- **Ladder setback ratio:** 1:4 (horizontal to vertical)[^1]
- **Ladder extension above landing:** minimum 1 m (3 ft)[^1]
- **Wire rope rejection (broken wire criterion):** 10% of wires broken per strand in one lay length[^2]
- **Cable clip minimum spacing:** 6× rope diameter[^2]
- **"Saddle to the dead end"** — clip orientation mnemonic[^2]

## What the textbook doesn't tell you

**Sling angle kills quietly.** A 2-leg sling at a 120° included angle (30° from horizontal) is being loaded to double what a casual glance suggests. Many load incidents aren't from overloaded slings in the vertical — they're from people rigging wide baskets at nearly flat angles without doing the angle math. Whenever you're picking something wider than it is tall, calculate.[^2]

**Synthetic slings and hot work don't mix, period.** A synthetic sling doesn't burst into flames — it melts gradually, silently, and then parts instantly. Journeypersons who have seen a sling failure won't let synthetics anywhere near a weld job. On welding sites, the default is wire rope or alloy chain.[^3]

**Plate clamps have a specific grain direction sensitivity.** Most plate clamps are designed to grip on plate that's oriented so the rolling grain is horizontal to the clamping jaws. Rotating the clamp 90° (lifting from the edge that ran perpendicular to the mill roll direction) can reduce grip. On thick plate (25 mm+), always consult the manufacturer's guidance on plate edge orientation.[^3]

**Re-torque your cable clips.** Crews apply clips, do a test lift, assume everything is fine, then load fully. The first lift relaxes the wire, loosening the clips. OSHA data shows a large proportion of cable clip failures happen because nobody re-torqued. Get in the habit every time, not just on "big" lifts.[^2]

## Key terms

- **Working Load Limit (WLL):** the maximum mass a rigging component is rated to lift in a given hitch configuration under normal working conditions
- **Proof test load:** typically 2× WLL — the load used to certify a new sling; not an operational figure
- **Breaking strength:** load that will destroy the sling — typically 5× WLL for synthetic; far beyond WLL; not an operational figure
- **Included angle:** the angle between two sling legs meeting at the hook or shackle — wider angle = more stress per leg
- **Dead end:** the short, non-load-bearing tail of a wire rope at a clip connection
- **Live end:** the load-bearing run of the rope
- **Choker hitch:** a self-tightening hitch formed by passing one end of the sling through its own eye
- **Basket hitch:** sling passed under the load with both eyes on the hook — doubles rated capacity when load is balanced
- **Centre of gravity (CG):** the point through which the entire weight of an object effectively acts
- **IWRC:** Independent Wire Rope Core — a wire rope with a steel wire core (vs fibre core); higher crush resistance, preferred for industrial lifting

## Common exam trap

- **Included angle vs leg angle:** exam questions give the included angle (between the two legs) and expect you to use the leg angle (from horizontal). They're not the same. A 90° included angle = 45° leg angle.
- **Choker hitch WLL:** distractors often state 67% or 80% — the standard value is **75%**. (Some manufacturers rate it slightly differently, but 75% is the Alberta-accepted figure.[^2])
- **"Saddle to the dead end" reversal:** classic distractor is a diagram with the U-bolt on the live end. Identify it. The saddle contacts the live (load-bearing) side.
- **Synthetic slings near heat:** distractors may say "nylon slings may be used if kept 150 mm from the weld zone" — there is no such allowable proximity. **Synthetic slings are not to be used in hot work environments.**
- **Wire rope rejection:** the criterion is not "any broken wire" — it's **10% broken within one lay length** of any strand. One broken wire in a 37-wire strand is not automatic rejection; 4 broken wires in a single strand might be.

## Practice question preview

**Q:** A two-leg bridle sling has a rated WLL of 4 000 kg per leg in a vertical hitch. The sling is rigged at an included angle of 120°. What is the maximum safe load the two-leg sling can lift?

A) 8 000 kg  
B) 5 680 kg  
C) 4 000 kg  
D) 2 830 kg  

**Correct: C**

**Explanation:** At 120° included angle, each sling leg makes a 30° angle with the horizontal. The load factor per leg = sin(30°) = 0.50. Each leg can therefore support 4 000 × 0.50 = 2 000 kg of vertical force. Two legs combined = 2 × 2 000 = **4 000 kg** total lift capacity. The answer isn't 8 000 kg (that would be a vertical, 0° included angle basket hitch) and isn't 5 680 kg (that's the 90°/45° case). Many students incorrectly add raw WLLs without accounting for sling angle — this is the most common rigging calculation trap on period exams.
