# Hazardous Materials and Fire Protection

A welding shop is one of the most flammable environments you'll ever work in — and most welding fires are preventable. Knowing the classes of fire and the difference between a fire watch and a hot work permit has kept welders employed (and alive).

---

## Hazardous material handling and storage

A hazardous material is any substance that can cause harm through chemical, physical, or biological properties. As a welder, you'll regularly handle:

- **Compressed gas cylinders** (oxygen, acetylene, argon, CO₂, propane, nitrogen, hydrogen)
- **Solvents and degreasers** (acetone, MEK, isopropyl alcohol)
- **Fluxes** (some are irritants; stainless steel flux may contain fluorides)
- **Paint, primer, and coatings** (some contain isocyanates or heavy metal pigments)
- **Spent electrodes and slag** (manganese, chromium, nickel in certain alloy consumables)

### Compressed gas cylinder rules

Cylinders are under high pressure — a valve breach on an oxygen cylinder turns it into a missile. Follow these rules without exception:[^2]

1. **Transport upright** and secured — chain or restrain to a proper cylinder cart. Never drag, drop, roll on side, or use the valve cap as a handle.
2. **Store separated by gas type** and by flammable vs. oxidizer category:
   - Oxygen cylinders and fuel gas cylinders (acetylene, propane) must be separated by a minimum **6 m (20 ft)** of open space OR a **1.5 m (5 ft) non-combustible barrier** when stored together.[^2]
   - Oxygen cylinders are oxidizers — they accelerate any combustion event. Never store near grease, oil, or open flames.
3. **Cap the valves** whenever cylinders are not in use. If a cylinder falls and the valve is off, the gas stays contained. Valve caps are not optional.
4. **Never apply grease or oil** to oxygen cylinders, regulators, or fittings — oxygen + hydrocarbon = explosion risk.
5. **Mark empty cylinders** "MT" or "EMPTY" and store separately. Pressure remaining in "empty" cylinders can still cause injury — treat empties like full ones until returned to the supplier.
6. **Cylinder temperature:** Do not store above **52°C (125°F)**. In Alberta summers, a cylinder baking in direct sunlight against a south-facing wall can exceed this. Shade cylinders or store inside.

### Solvents and liquid hazardous materials

- **Read the SDS** before using any new solvent — particularly Section 7 (handling/storage) and Section 8 (PPE required).
- Use the **minimum quantity needed.** Don't decant a full drum into open pails sitting around a hot area.
- **Grounding:** when transferring flammable solvents between containers, bond both containers together and ground to earth before pouring. Static sparks from liquid transfer have caused fires.
- Store flammable liquids in approved **flammable storage cabinets** (FM-approved, typically bright yellow, vented) — not in regular steel shelving or under open flame equipment.

## Venting procedures for hazardous materials

Venting is about removing flammable vapour, toxic fumes, or asphyxiant gases before they reach dangerous levels.

### When venting is required

- **Enclosed spaces:** before entering any enclosed area where gas could have accumulated (pipe segments, tanks, pressure vessels, crawl spaces) — test the atmosphere, do not rely on smell alone. Gas monitors are required equipment for confined space entry.[^3]
- **Solvent evaporation areas:** any area where flammable solvents are used must have sufficient ventilation to prevent vapour accumulation above 25% of the Lower Explosive Limit (LEL). Concentrations above the LEL range are explosive; below 25% of LEL is the accepted safe working level.
- **After cylinder incidents:** if a cylinder is leaking in an enclosed space, evacuate first, ventilate, then address the leak — never close a leaking valve in a gassed-up space with ignition sources present.

### Ventilation methods

- **Natural ventilation** (openings, doors, ridge vents): adequate only in large, open shop environments where crossflow is confirmed. Not adequate for enclosed spaces.
- **Forced air ventilation** (blowers/fans): move fresh air into the work area, pushing contaminated air out. Position supply blower to blow fresh air toward the worker, not to pull contaminated air past the worker.
- **Local exhaust ventilation (LEV):** extraction hoods or flexible hoses capturing fumes at the source. Preferred for welding fumes. Alberta OHS Code requires LEV when general ventilation cannot maintain exposures below TLVs.[^3]

## Fire hazards and fire classes

### Fire triangle and fire tetrahedron

Every fire needs three things simultaneously:

1. **Fuel** — combustible material (wood, paper, cloth, grease, acetylene, natural gas)
2. **Oxygen** — at least 16% concentration in air (normal air = 21%)
3. **Heat (ignition)** — welding arc, grinder sparks, hot metal surface

Fire prevention means removing **at least one** of these. In welding, you often can't remove the heat source, so you remove the fuel (clearance) and control oxygen.

### Fire classes (Canadian system)

You will be tested on these:[^1]

| Class | Fuel type | Example | Correct extinguisher |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Ordinary combustibles | Wood, paper, cloth, rubber | Water, foam, ABC dry chemical, CO₂ |
| **B** | Flammable/combustible liquids and gases | Gasoline, propane, acetylene, solvents | CO₂, dry chemical (ABC or BC), foam |
| **C** | Energized electrical equipment | Switchgear, live panels, arc-flash | CO₂, dry chemical — **NO water** |
| **D** | Combustible metals | Magnesium, titanium, sodium, lithium | Class D dry powder (specific to metal) |

> **Class K** (kitchen cooking oils/fats) exists but is rarely tested in welder programs.

**Welding fires are almost always Class B or Class A.** A welding fire starting in structural steel insulation, packing material, or wooden forms is Class A. A fire in an acetylene hose or oil reservoir is Class B.

### Hot work permits and fire watch

The **hot work permit** is a written authorization signed by a supervisor or safety officer, required before performing any open-flame or spark-producing work in areas with potential fire hazards. NFPA 51B and many industrial sites mandate these.[^1]

Contents of a hot work permit typically include:
- Date and time window authorized
- Location and description of the work
- Identification of hazards in the area
- Precautions taken (removed combustibles, wetted surfaces, fire watch assigned)
- Signature of authorizing person

**Fire watch requirements (NFPA 51B):**[^1]
- A trained fire watch must be present **during hot work and for a minimum 30 minutes after work stops**.
- The fire watch must be equipped with a fire extinguisher and have unobstructed access to it.
- The fire watch must be free to do nothing but watch for fire — they cannot also be helping weld.
- If combustibles exist on the **opposite side of the partition** being welded on (e.g., the other side of a metal wall with wood studs), a fire watch is required **on both sides**.

**Clearance distance:**
- Remove or protect all combustibles within **10 m (35 ft)** of the hot work area.[^1] [^2]
- If combustibles cannot be moved (structural members, built-in insulation), wet down, cover with welding blanket rated for the purpose, or move the hot work.

### Fire extinguisher classes and ratings

Alberta OHS Code requires fire extinguishers be available where fire hazards exist. Reading the label:

- **Numerical rating before A:** number × 1.25 litres of water equivalent capacity against Class A fires. A "2A" extinguisher handles 2 × 1.25 = 2.5 L equivalent.
- **Numerical rating before B:** area (in sq ft) that can be protected against Class B/C fires. A "40B" extinguisher can protect against a 40 sq ft flammable liquid fire.
- **C rating:** no number — just the letter, meaning the agent is non-conductive (safe on energized equipment).

Common in welding shops: **ABC 10 lb dry chemical** (the red one) or **5 lb CO₂** (for electrical panel work).[^3]

**CO₂ extinguishers do not work well outdoors in wind** — the agent disperses. Dry chemical is better outdoors.

## Numbers you need to memorize

- **Oxygen/fuel cylinder storage separation:** 6 m (20 ft) of open space OR 1.5 m (5 ft) non-combustible barrier[^2]
- **Cylinder storage temperature max:** 52°C (125°F)[^2]
- **Fire watch minimum duration after hot work stops:** 30 minutes[^1]
- **Combustible clearance radius:** 10 m (35 ft) from hot work[^1] [^2]
- **Minimum oxygen level for fire:** 16% (normal air = 21% O₂)
- **Safe vapour concentration:** < 25% of LEL (Lower Explosive Limit) for flammable vapours[^3]
- **Fire class A:** ordinary combustibles (wood, paper, cloth)
- **Fire class B:** flammable liquids and gases
- **Fire class C:** energized electrical equipment — **NO water**
- **Fire class D:** combustible metals (mag, titanium, sodium) — Class D powder only

## What the textbook doesn't tell you

**Fire watches are killed by shift change.** The most dangerous moment in a hot work operation is when the fire watch changes shifts or goes for coffee. Fires in concealed voids (between walls, under insulation) can smoulder for 20–40 minutes before breaking out. A 30-minute fire watch means 30 continuous minutes with eyes on the area — not 30 minutes in total with gaps.[^1]

**Acetylene is uniquely dangerous.** Unlike propane or natural gas, acetylene becomes unstable at pressures above **103 kPa gauge (15 PSI gauge)** — it can decompose and explode without air or oxygen present. This is why you should never open an acetylene cylinder valve more than **1.5 turns** and why acetylene must never be stored or used in the horizontal position (the acetone carrier solvent must cover the porous media).[^2]

**"Empty" cylinders injure people.** A cylinder that registers zero gauge pressure still contains residual gas — especially at low temperatures where reading drops. Return cylinders promptly to the supplier and do not use "empty" cylinders for non-welding purposes (cribbing, weights, etc.).[^2]

**CO₂ extinguishers don't last long.** A standard 5 lb CO₂ extinguisher discharges fully in about 8–10 seconds. If you hesitate, retreat, and re-approach, it will be empty when you need it. On an electrical fire in a panel, commit, discharge fully, and confirm before you stop.[^3]

## Key terms

- **Hot work permit:** written authorization to perform spark- or flame-producing work in a potentially hazardous area
- **Fire watch:** a designated person whose sole duty is to watch for fire during and after hot work
- **LEL (Lower Explosive Limit):** the minimum vapour concentration in air at which ignition can occur
- **UEL (Upper Explosive Limit):** the maximum concentration above which ignition cannot occur (too rich to burn)
- **Flammable liquid:** flash point below 37.8°C (100°F)
- **Combustible liquid:** flash point at or above 37.8°C but below 93°C (200°F)
- **Oxidizer:** a substance that releases oxygen to support combustion — oxygen cylinders are oxidizers
- **Class A/B/C/D fire:** classification of fire by fuel type, governing which extinguishant to use
- **ABC dry chemical:** multipurpose extinguishing agent (monoammonium phosphate) — works on Class A, B, and C fires

## Common exam trap

- **Fire class C = energized electrical:** distractors love to include "CO₂ is best for Class C" (true) and "water is acceptable for Class C at low voltage" (absolutely false — water conducts electricity and kills the operator).
- **30-minute fire watch vs "until the area cools":** the specific number is **30 minutes**. Vague language ("until safe") is a distractor.
- **Acetylene pressure limit:** 103 kPa gauge (15 PSI gauge). Distractors often give 30 PSI, 25 PSI, or "oxygen regulator pressure" — wrong. 15 PSI / 103 kPa gauge is the maximum working pressure for acetylene.
- **Cylinder separation:** 6 m OR 1.5 m barrier. Both conditions are alternatives — you don't need both. Exam may ask which is correct when given only the barrier option: that **is** acceptable.
- **Flammable vs combustible liquids:** the flash point cutoff is 37.8°C (100°F). Acetone flash point is −20°C — flammable. Diesel flash point is ~52°C — combustible. Distractors blur this.

## Practice question preview

**Q:** A welder completes structural welding inside a metal building at 16:00. A fire watch is assigned during the work. At what time can the fire watch be released?

A) When the welder packs up equipment and leaves the area  
B) Immediately after the last arc is struck  
C) 30 minutes after the last spark-producing operation ends  
D) When the metal surface cools to touch temperature  

**Correct: C**

**Explanation:** NFPA 51B requires a trained fire watch to remain for **30 minutes minimum after hot work operations cease**. Fires in concealed spaces (insulation, wall cavities, hollow structural sections) often take 20–40 minutes to develop into visible flame. "Touch temperature" has no defined threshold and is a distractor. The welder's departure time is irrelevant — what matters is when the last arc was struck.
