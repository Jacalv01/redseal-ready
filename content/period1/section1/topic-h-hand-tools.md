# Hand Tools

You cannot weld accurately what you haven't marked and measured accurately. A journeyperson who can lay out a flange plate to ±0.5 mm with a scriber and square is worth more than one who can "eyeball" everything within 5 mm. These tools are the foundation of every piece of work that leaves your hands.

---

## Hand tool safety precautions

Alberta OHS Code Part 19 applies to all hand and power tools on a worksite.[^1]

General rules that apply to all hand tools:
- **Use the right tool for the right job.** Using a file as a pry bar, a screwdriver as a chisel, or a combination square as a hammer creates damaged tools and flying metal.
- **Inspect before use:** cracked handles, loose hammer heads, dull cutting edges, mushroomed chisel heads (the "mushroom" on a hardened tool can shatter and become a projectile).
- **Mushroomed tools (chisels, punches, drifts):** when the struck end of a hardened tool mushrooms, the overhanging material can fly off when struck. Dress (grind) the struck end to restore a proper chamfer before reuse.[^1]
- **Keep cutting tools sharp:** a dull tool requires more force and is more likely to slip.
- **Secure the workpiece:** use a vise, clamp, or fixture — never hold small workpieces by hand while filing, chiseling, or scraping. The cut or chip that misses the work lands in your palm.
- **Handle pointed tools (scribers, awls, punches):** cap or store pointed end down in a holder — a loose scriber rolling off a bench is a puncture hazard.
- **Eye protection:** always when chipping, chiseling, striking hardened tools, or using a hammer near any metal surfaces.

## Layout and measuring tools

### Steel rule (scale)

A rigid graduated scale in mm/cm or imperial inches. Key accuracy points:[^2]

- Read from the end, not from a worn or damaged graduation mark
- Hook rules (with a hooked end) allow self-referencing measurement from an edge
- **Graduated in 1/16 in. (most common), 1/32 in. (finer work), or mm**
- Do not use as a screwdriver or pry bar — rules bow and lose calibration

### Tape measure (steel tape)

Retractable steel tape, 3–8 m common in welding shops. The hook at the end is **designed to float** — it slides in/out by exactly the thickness of the hook itself. This is correct — it compensates for inside and outside measurements (the hook moves in for outside, moves out for inside). A bent or stuck hook causes every measurement to be off by the hook thickness.[^2]

- For accurate structural layout: measure twice from the same datum, not from multiple intermediate points (accumulated error).

### Combination square

A combination square consists of a **steel rule (blade)** and a **stock (body)** that slides and locks anywhere on the blade. The stock has two reference surfaces: a **90° face** (for scribing perpendicular lines, checking squareness) and a **45° face** (for scribing mitre angles). Most also contain a small **spirit level** in the stock and a **scriber stored in the stock end**.[^2]

Uses:
- Mark lines perpendicular to a plate edge
- Mark 45° lines
- Check squareness of assembled pieces
- Measure depth of recesses (set blade depth, lock, transfer)

**Precision check:** place the stock on a flat surface and scribe a line with the blade vertical. Flip the square 180° and check the blade against the scribed line. If they align, the square is true. If not, it's out of square — don't use for precision layout.

### Try square (carpenter's square / framing square)

Large fixed-angle L-shape, 16 in. × 24 in. (large framing square) or 6 in. × 4 in. (small try square). Used for checking and marking right angles on large material, and for squaring assemblies.[^2]

### Bevel gauge (bevel transferer / sliding T-bevel)

Adjustable blade that locks to any angle — used to transfer and repeat a specific angle from one piece to another. Common use: transferring the bevel angle from a drawing (or a protractor) to a workpiece without needing to repeatedly measure the angle.[^2]

### Protractor

Measures angles directly. Combination square sets often include a protractor head that replaces the 90/45° stock, reading directly in degrees.[^2]

### Scriber

A hardened steel pointed tool used to mark lines on metal. Produces a fine, precise line in metal surfaces (unlike pencil, which is lost in grinding dust). Preferred over pencil for layout lines that must be accurate.[^2]

### Punch tools

- **Centre punch:** hardened steel, pointed at ~90°. Used to mark a centre point for drilling — the divot guides the drill bit and prevents it from walking. Strike with a hammer, once, at the marked intersection.
- **Automatic centre punch:** spring-loaded — press down on the mark to strike without a hammer. Good for consistent force.
- **Prick punch:** sharper point (~60°), lighter strike — for marking layout lines that will be followed by scribing or further layout work. Not for drill starts.
- **Pin punch:** parallel-sided (no taper). Used to drive out pins from assemblies without widening the hole.[^2]

### Dividers and trammel points

- **Dividers:** compass-like tool with two hardened steel legs — used to scribe arcs and circles on metal, and to divide distances into equal parts (step off equal intervals).
- **Trammel points:** large-scale version — two adjustable pointed tools mounted on a beam. Used for scribing large-radius arcs that are beyond the reach of a divider.

## Clamping tools

### C-clamp (G-clamp)

The most common shop clamp. Adjust the threaded screw to apply clamping force. Load rating varies by size and rating — do not use as a lifting device or substitute for structural fasteners. Apply pads between the clamp contact surfaces and the finished work if marking is a concern.[^2]

### Locking pliers (Vise-Grips)

Adjustable jaw pliers that lock onto the workpiece. The locking mechanism holds without constant hand pressure. Used for:
- Temporary tack-hold during fit-up
- Holding pipe square to a flange while tacking
- Gripping rounded or awkward shapes

**Caution:** locking pliers are not rated for suspension — do not use to hold a suspended load. They are a one-hand tool only.

### Pipe clamps / chain pipe clamps

Purpose-built clamps for holding round pipe sections for cut, fit-up, and tacking. Chain-type clamps wrap around the pipe and cinch tight.[^2]

### Beam clamps / strong-backs

Straight steel bars clamped or tack-welded across a joint to prevent angular distortion during welding. Pulled off after weld cools. The strong-back fights the angular shrinkage force.

### Magnetic clamps (welding magnets)

Permanent magnets rated for holding force (typically 11 kg / 25 lb to over 90 kg / 200 lb). Used to hold plate at 45°, 90°, and other fixed angles for tacking. Advantages: fast to apply, no tools needed. Disadvantages:[^2]

- Lose holding force near the weld arc (magnetic flux from the arc can partially demagnetize or displace a weak magnet — keep strong magnets out of the arc zone)
- Attract spatter and debris — clean magnets regularly
- Not suitable as sole restraint for any loaded position

### Come-alongs and chain falls (chain hoists)

Mechanical advantage lifting devices — used to pull misaligned members into position for fit-up. Not hand tools in the strictest sense, but included here as part of the welder's manipulation toolkit for fit-up work.

## Cutting hand tools

### Hacksaws

Frame-mounted blade for cutting metal by hand. Key points:[^2]

- **Blade TPI (teeth per inch):** 14–18 TPI for thick soft metal; 24–32 TPI for thin-wall tube and sheet. Rule: **minimum 3 teeth in contact with the workpiece at all times** — if the blade pitch is greater than the material thickness, teeth straddle the work and strip out.
- Cut on the **forward stroke** — tension on the frame keeps the blade taut. Ease pressure on the return stroke.
- Thin sheet and tube: use a finer blade and cut at a low angle (nearly flat) to maximize teeth contact.

### Files

Single-cut or double-cut, in various profiles (flat, half-round, round, square, triangular). Used to deburr cut edges, dress weld toes, and fit pieces.[^2]

**Bastard cut (coarser) → second cut → smooth cut (finest)** — the standard progression.
- Bastard for fast material removal
- Smooth for finish work and deburring

**File safety:** always use a file with a **handle** — a file without a handle that catches or slips can drive the tang through your palm.

### Cold chisels

Hardened steel flat-edged tools for:
- Chipping weld slag (though a chipping hammer is preferred)
- Cutting soft metal in limited applications
- Driving pins and removing seized bolts

**Always inspect the struck end.** A mushroomed chisel can shatter — grind the mushroom off before use.[^1]

### Chipping hammers

Specific to welding — used to remove weld slag from SMAW and FCAW welds. Typically has a pointed end (for removing slag from tight areas) and a chisel end. Chip along the bead, not into it — you're breaking the glass-like slag layer, not gouging the weld. Wear eye protection every time.[^2]

### Drift pins (drift punches)

Tapered steel pins used to align holes in structural members (bring bolt holes into alignment for fasteners). Do not use a standard punch as a drift pin — the taper and hardness are different.

## Other hand tools used by welders

### Wire brushes (hand brushes and cup brushes)

Steel wire brushes for cleaning weld beads, removing loose rust, and prepping surfaces. Two rules:[^2]
- Wear eye protection — wire tips fly off brushes.
- Use a **stainless steel wire brush** for stainless steel (and for cleaning between passes on stainless and aluminum) — a carbon steel brush contaminates the surface with iron particles that cause corrosion and fusion problems.

### Soapstone and chalk

Soapstone (talc) sticks are the standard marking tool for steel that will be cut or welded. They survive heat, produce a visible line on both clean and rusty steel, and burn off clean without contamination. **Never use a wax crayon or oil-based marker on weld joint surfaces** — they introduce carbon contamination.[^2]

### Electrode holder (stinger)

The SMAW electrode holder. Inspected for:
- Jaw tension (should grip electrode firmly without wobble)
- Insulation integrity — cracked insulation creates shock hazard
- Clean jaw faces (carbon and slag deposits increase contact resistance and cause overheating)

### Work clamp (ground clamp)

The return connection to the workpiece. Inspected for:
- Clean copper jaw faces (corroded jaw faces increase resistance and cause arc interruptions)
- Cable connection integrity
- Adequate cable size for amperage used

## Numbers you need to memorize

- **Hacksaw TPI rule:** minimum **3 teeth in contact** with the work at all times — guides blade selection[^2]
- **Common hacksaw TPI range:** 14–18 TPI (thick stock) to 24–32 TPI (thin wall)[^2]
- **Combination square angles:** 90° and **45°** (the two reference surfaces)[^2]
- **Centre punch point angle:** ~90° (compared to prick punch ~60°)[^2]
- **Tape measure hook float:** designed to move exactly the thickness of the hook — correct, not a defect[^2]
- **File progression:** bastard (coarse) → second cut → smooth (fine)[^2]

## What the textbook doesn't tell you

**The combination square is the most underused layout tool on job sites.** Journeypersons who pull out a combination square for every T-joint layout, perpendicular mark, and fit-up check are measurably more accurate than those who eyeball it. A 1° angle error on a 300 mm fillet arm projects to 5 mm of misalignment at the tip. Spend the 10 seconds to check with the square.[^2]

**A stainless steel brush is not optional on stainless and aluminum.** Using a carbon steel wire brush on stainless steel wire-brushes iron into the surface — those iron particles corrode, stain the finished surface, and reduce corrosion resistance. On aluminum, iron contamination at the weld zone causes fusion problems. Keep a dedicated stainless brush. Mark it. Don't let it get used on mild steel.[^2]

**Mushroom a chisel, it can kill someone across the shop.** Hardened steel mushroom caps have been documented as projectiles in workplace incidents. A chisel struck with a hammer that has a mushroomed struck end can send a fragment of hardened steel spinning across a shop at velocities that pierce PPE. This is why the OHS Code requires inspection and dressing of striking tools — it's not a paperwork exercise.[^1]

**The tape hook float error is the most common measuring mistake.** Apprentices who either pull the hook back (compressing it) or push it too far (extending it) when measuring get readings that are off by 1–2 mm consistently. The hook is supposed to slide freely the thickness of the hook material. If it doesn't slide freely, the tape is damaged — replace it.[^2]

## Key terms

- **Layout:** the process of transferring dimensional information from drawings onto the actual material — marking cut lines, hole centres, and assembly references
- **Datum:** a reference surface or line from which all measurements are taken — avoids accumulated measurement error
- **Scriber:** hardened steel pointed tool for marking fine lines on metal
- **Prick punch:** sharpened punch for marking layout line points; lighter than centre punch
- **Centre punch:** creates a starting divot for a drill bit — heavier mark than prick punch
- **Hacksaw TPI:** teeth per inch — determines which material thickness can be cut efficiently
- **Bastard file:** coarse-cut file for rapid material removal
- **Mushroomed tool:** a striking tool (chisel, punch) where the struck end has spread outward — dangerous, must be dressed (ground) before use
- **Strong-back:** straight bar clamped or tack-welded across a joint to resist angular distortion

## Common exam trap

- **Combination square angles:** it provides **90° and 45°** — not 60°. Distractors may claim it provides a 60° face. It does not; a bevel gauge is needed for arbitrary angles.
- **Tape measure hook:** the floating hook is **correct design** — not a defect or a reason to discard the tape. Distractors claim the hook should be rigid.
- **Hacksaw TPI selection:** a 14 TPI blade on 1.5 mm (16-gauge) sheet will straddle the material (fewer than 3 teeth in contact) — wrong answer. For thin material, 32 TPI is correct.
- **File safety:** "A file may be used without a handle for short jobs" — false. Always use a handle. The tang can penetrate the palm if the file catches.
- **Stainless brush vs carbon steel brush:** any question about cleaning stainless steel or aluminum weld zones — the correct answer always specifies **stainless steel wire brush** specifically.
- **Centre punch vs prick punch:** centre punch = drill start (90° point); prick punch = layout line (60° point). Swapping these in exam answers is a common distractor.

## Practice question preview

**Q:** A welder is preparing to cut a 300 mm × 300 mm square from a piece of 3 mm (10-gauge) sheet metal using a hacksaw. Which hacksaw blade should they select, and why?

A) 14 TPI — coarser teeth remove material faster on steel  
B) 18 TPI — adequate tooth count for steel cutting, general purpose  
C) 32 TPI — minimum 3 teeth must contact the 3 mm material at all times  
D) Any TPI is acceptable for sheet metal — only pressure matters  

**Correct: C**

**Explanation:** The rule for hacksaw blade selection is that **at least 3 teeth must be in contact with the workpiece at all times**. A hacksaw tooth pitch at 14 TPI = approximately 1.8 mm per tooth spacing. Three teeth span 3 × 1.8 mm = 5.4 mm — greater than the 3 mm sheet thickness. At 14 TPI, fewer than 3 teeth contact the sheet, causing the blade to straddle the work, teeth catch and strip, and the cut becomes rough and uncontrolled. At 32 TPI, tooth spacing = ~0.8 mm, and 3 teeth cover 2.4 mm — less than the 3 mm thickness, so 3+ teeth are always in contact. Option D is false — blade selection is the primary factor for thin material.
