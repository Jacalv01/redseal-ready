# Welding Safety

The welding arc doesn't care if you're a first-day apprentice or a 20-year journeyperson — UV radiation, toxic fumes, electric shock, and confined space asphyxiation kill without bias. Understanding the hazards and controls isn't a box to tick; it's how you make it to retirement.

---

## Hazards in welding and cutting operations

Every time you strike an arc or light a torch, you're working around multiple simultaneous hazards:

| Hazard | Mechanism | Affected area |
|---|---|---|
| UV/IR radiation | Arc emits intense ultraviolet and infrared — arc flash burns corneas and skin | Eyes, face, exposed skin |
| Electric shock | Arc welders operate at high amperages with potentially hazardous open circuit voltages | Heart, muscles, skin |
| Fumes and gases | Metal vapours (manganese, chromium, nickel, lead), shielding gas displaces O₂ | Lungs, nervous system |
| Fire/explosion | Sparks travel up to 10 m; hot metal and ignition sources interact | Surrounding area |
| Noise | Grinding, arc gouging, plasma cutting exceed 85 dBA | Hearing |
| Falls | Working elevated on plate, scaffolding, ladders while welding | Whole body |
| Heat stress | FR clothing + arc heat + summer temps | Cardiovascular system |
| Crush/pinch | Material handling, positioning jigs | Hands, fingers |

## PPE selection for welding and cutting

### Eye and face protection — the most critical

The welding arc emits radiation across the UV, visible, and IR spectrum. Without the correct filter shade, a welder suffers **photokeratitis** (arc eye / welder's flash) — essentially a sunburn on the corneas — within minutes, with symptoms appearing 6–12 hours later.[^3]

**Filter shade selection (CSA Z94.3):**[^3]

| Process | Typical current range | Minimum shade | Recommended shade |
|---|---|---|---|
| SMAW (Stick) | 75–200 A | 10 | 11–12 |
| SMAW (Stick) | 200–400 A | 12 | 12–13 |
| GMAW/FCAW | 60–160 A | 10 | 11 |
| GMAW/FCAW | 160–250 A | 10 | 12 |
| GMAW/FCAW | 250–500 A | 10 | 14 |
| GTAW | < 50 A | 8 | 10 |
| GTAW | 50–150 A | 8 | 12 |
| GTAW | 150–500 A | 10 | 14 |
| Plasma cutting (PAC) | 300–400 A | 8 | 9 |
| Oxyfuel cutting | n/a (no arc) | 3–4 | 5 (goggles required) |
| Carbon arc gouging | 150–500+ A | 14 | 14 |

**Adjacent workers** (helpers, grinders working nearby) must also be protected from arc flash — use welding screens/curtains (opaque or filter-tinted) to block stray arc radiation.[^1]

**Auto-darkening helmets:** allowed and widely used. They must darken before the arc starts — the switch-on time must be **≤ 1/3 500 second (< 0.3 ms)** for it to be safe. Cheap auto-dark helmets with sluggish response times cause arc eye even though they look like they work.[^3]

### Body protection

- **Clothing:** flame-resistant (FR) — wool, leather, or purpose-made FR cotton. No synthetic fibres (polyester, nylon) — they melt onto skin. Roll down sleeves; do not leave collar open — spatter burns heal slowly.
- **Leather gloves:** required for SMAW, GMAW, FCAW, and all cutting. For GTAW, lighter gloves allow better torch feel — but still leather.
- **Leather apron or jacket:** for overhead work or heavy position welding where spatter falls onto you.
- **Safety footwear:** CSA Z195 Grade 1 (green triangle) — steel-toed, puncture-resistant. No running shoes, no sneakers, no open toes.
- **Hearing protection:** required when ambient noise exceeds **85 dBA**. Angle grinding is typically 95–105 dBA — hearing protection every time, no exceptions.[^2]

## Welding fumes and gases — hazards and controls

Welding fumes are a complex mixture of metal oxides, silicates, and fluorides from the electrode, flux coating, base metal, and coatings. The specific hazards depend on what's being welded.[^1]

### Common hazardous fume components

| Fume/gas | Source | Health effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Manganese (Mn)** | Most steel electrodes (E7018, GMAW wire) | Cumulative neurotoxin — causes Parkinson's-like symptoms (manganism) with chronic overexposure |
| **Hexavalent chromium Cr(VI)** | Stainless steel welding (304, 316) | Confirmed human carcinogen — lung cancer; airway sensitizer |
| **Nickel compounds** | Stainless steel, Inconel | Probable carcinogen — respiratory tract |
| **Lead fumes** | Welding/cutting leaded steel, leaded paint | CNS damage, reproductive toxin |
| **Ozone (O₃)** | Generated by UV from GMAW/GTAW arcs | Pulmonary irritant — worse in confined spaces |
| **Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ)** | Any high-temperature arc process | Pulmonary edema (delayed onset — can be fatal) |
| **Carbon monoxide (CO)** | Incomplete combustion; CO₂ shielding gas at high heat | Asphyxiant — binds hemoglobin, prevents O₂ transport |

### Controls — hierarchy

Follow the **hierarchy of controls** — don't jump straight to respirators as the only solution:[^2]

1. **Elimination:** don't weld coated, galvanized, or painted metal without stripping the coating first in a ventilated area.
2. **Substitution:** use a lower-fume-generating process where possible (e.g., GTAW produces fewer fumes than SMAW for the same joint).
3. **Engineering controls:** local exhaust ventilation (LEV) — fume extraction guns (GMAW) or fixed extraction hoods positioned within 150–300 mm of the arc. General shop ventilation (at least 2,000 m³ of air per kg of electrode consumed, per ACGIH guideline — check your local OHS code requirements).
4. **Administrative controls:** keep your head out of the fume plume. The plume rises directly above the arc — tilt your head slightly to the side, or work cross-handed. Rotate workers to limit exposure time.
5. **PPE (last line):** air-purifying respirators (N95/P100 for particulate, appropriate cartridges for acid gases/organics) or supplied air when LEV is not feasible. For stainless or hexavalent chromium work, respiratory protection is typically mandatory regardless of ventilation.[^2]

**Rule of thumb:** if you can smell the fume, the controls are failing — the nose detects welding fumes at concentrations well below some OELs, but above zero is still above zero.

## Electrical safety

### The physics of electric shock

At welding amperages and voltages, electric shock can cause:[^1]

- **Ventricular fibrillation** (heart stops pumping in a coordinated way) — as little as **50 mA through the chest** can trigger V-fib; 100 mA is typically lethal if it crosses the heart.
- **Burns** — both entry and exit wounds, often severe.
- **Muscle tetany** — current locks muscles; a gripped electrode holder cannot be released (the victim cannot let go).

Common welding machine open-circuit voltages:
- DC SMAW: **40–80 V OCV** (Open Circuit Voltage)
- AC SMAW: **50–90 V OCV**
- GMAW: **15–35 V OCV**
- GTAW: **60–80 V OCV (with HF start)**

These voltages are not "safe" — wet skin, contact with two live conductors, or ground faults create lethal conditions at these levels.[^1]

### Electrical safety precautions

1. **Inspect cables before every use** — cracked insulation, bare conductors, and damaged electrode holders are immediate remove-from-service conditions.
2. **Never wrap electrode cables around your body** — if the machine cycles or you slip, the cable can act as a conductor to ground through you.
3. **Keep the work return clamp close to the weld.** A long stray current path through structural steel, pipe runs, or building frames damages bearings, control boards, and other metallic components through stray current heating. Always clamp directly to the workpiece.[^1]
4. **Do not weld in wet or damp conditions** without elevated safety precautions — wet clothing reduces skin resistance dramatically, making lower voltages lethal.
5. **Do not change electrodes with bare hands** — use dry leather gloves. Never touch the bare stump of an electrode with bare fingers while the machine is on.
6. **Switch off machine before adjusting internals** — electrode holders, drive rolls, gas connections, terminal blocks. Machine off = safe. "Just quickly" is how accidents happen.
7. **Ground the workpiece** — the work return conductor must form a complete circuit. A floating workpiece with a single conductor is a shock hazard to anyone who touches the work and a second conductor simultaneously.
8. For GTAW with **high-frequency start (HF):** HF energy can arc through light gloves and affect implanted cardiac devices. Workers with pacemakers should consult their physician before working near HF start GTAW equipment.[^1]

## Confined space welding and cutting

### What is a confined space?

Alberta OHS Code Part 5 defines a confined space as an enclosed or partially enclosed space that:[^2]

1. Is large enough for a worker to enter and perform work
2. Has limited means of entry and exit
3. Is not designed for continuous worker occupancy

For welders: pipe systems, pressure vessels, tanks, manholes, boiler fireboxes, storage tanks, trenches more than 1.2 m deep, and many utility vaults qualify.

### Hazards specific to confined space welding

- **Oxygen-deficient atmosphere:** shielding gases (argon, CO₂) are heavier than air and displace oxygen in low-lying spaces. As little as **19.5% O₂** triggers mandatory entry procedures; below **16%** causes unconsciousness rapidly.
- **Flammable atmosphere:** residue from previous contents (hydrocarbons, solvents) can create explosive vapour concentrations. Hot work in such an atmosphere is prohibited until the space is cleaned, purged, and tested.
- **Toxic atmosphere:** fumes from coatings inside tanks, welding in galvanized or lead-painted vessels, organic residues from food tanks — all create toxic atmospheres in confined spaces.
- **Heat accumulation:** a confined space has little ventilation; arc heat and fume temperatures build rapidly, creating heat stress conditions.

### Confined space entry procedure[^2]

1. **Hazard identification:** What was in the space? What coatings are present? What gases could be present?
2. **Atmosphere testing:** Test oxygen (target: 20.9%, minimum 19.5%), LEL (must be <10% LEL before entry, <25% to continue work), and toxic gases (CO, H₂S) with a calibrated multi-gas monitor — **before** entering, and continuously during work.
3. **Hot work permit:** mandatory for all confined space hot work.
4. **Entry permit:** signed document listing the hazards, controls, atmospheric test results, entrant names, attendant name, and rescue plan.
5. **Attendant:** a trained standby person outside the confined space at all times workers are inside. The attendant does NOT enter to rescue — they alert emergency services.
6. **Retrieval system:** lifeline or tripod-and-winch for non-permit-required spaces; full rescue provisions for permit-required.
7. **Continuous forced-air ventilation:** introduce fresh air continuously during welding. The ventilation source must be clean-air (not boosted with oxygen — boosting with oxygen creates an oxygen-enriched atmosphere that makes everything flammable).
8. **Scheduled breaks:** exit the confined space periodically — fume accumulation accelerates faster than the worker often perceives.

> **Critical:** Never use oxygen to purge or "sweeten" a confined space. Oxygen-enriched atmospheres (above 23%) make clothing, grease, and cardboard spontaneously flammable. **Air only for ventilation.**[^1]

## Numbers you need to memorize

- **Arc eye (photokeratitis):** caused by UV exposure — symptoms appear **6–12 hours** after exposure
- **Auto-darkening helmet switch speed:** must be **< 0.3 ms (1/3 500 second)** to prevent arc eye[^3]
- **Minimum shade for SMAW 75–200 A:** Shade **10** (minimum), 11–12 recommended[^3]
- **Noise action level:** **85 dBA** — hearing protection required at or above this level[^2]
- **Lethal chest current:** approximately **50–100 mA** through the heart[^1]
- **Typical SMAW OCV:** 40–80 V DC; 50–90 V AC[^1]
- **Minimum O₂ for confined space entry:** **19.5%** (normal = 20.9%)[^2]
- **Maximum LEL for confined space hot work:** < **10% LEL** to enter; maintain < **25% LEL** during work[^2]
- **Confined space attendant rule:** one trained attendant **outside at all times** — does NOT enter to rescue[^2]
- **Oxygen never for ventilation:** above **23% O₂** = oxygen-enriched atmosphere — everything becomes a fire risk[^1]

## What the textbook doesn't tell you

**Arc eye severity is deceptive.** When you get flashed, you feel nothing for hours. By midnight, it feels like someone poured sand in your eyes and then shone a searchlight at them. Workers dismiss early flashes as minor — they're not. Cumulative UV exposure also increases long-term cataract risk.[^3]

**Your neck is the gap in your PPE.** Most welders cover their face with a helmet and put on gloves and boots, then forget that their neck is exposed and at face level with the arc when welding flat. A quick pulse of UV on an uncovered neck (open collar) burns exactly like arc eye on skin. Button up or flip your collar up before you start.

**The return clamp location matters electrically.** If you clamp the work return to the shop floor table and then weld on a piece of pipe that's only touching the table through paint, the return path may include building metalwork, creating stray current that erodes bearings in motors and instruments elsewhere in the shop. Clamp directly to the work being welded.[^1]

**CO from CO₂ shielding gas.** At the arc temperatures reached during GMAW, CO₂ dissociates: CO₂ → CO + O. The CO recombines after the arc in open air, but in confined spaces the CO concentration can reach hazardous levels. GMAW in confined spaces always requires CO monitoring — not just LEL and O₂.[^5]

## Key terms

- **Photokeratitis (arc eye / welder's flash):** UV burn of the cornea — same mechanism as snow blindness
- **Open circuit voltage (OCV):** the voltage between the electrode and work clamp when no arc is present; the idle voltage that a worker can be exposed to when changing electrodes
- **Ventricular fibrillation (V-fib):** chaotic, uncoordinated heart rhythm that stops effective pumping — the mechanism of death from electric shock through the chest
- **Confined space:** any enclosed or partially enclosed space with restricted entry/exit, not designed for continuous occupancy
- **Permit-required confined space:** a confined space that has one or more serious hazards (toxic/flammable atmosphere, engulfment, entrapment risk, or other recognized hazard)
- **LEL (Lower Explosive Limit):** minimum vapour concentration at which ignition can occur
- **Attendant:** trained person stationed outside a confined space while workers are inside
- **LEV (Local Exhaust Ventilation):** point-of-capture fume extraction — hoods, extraction guns, flexible ducts
- **OCV (Open Circuit Voltage):** see above
- **Hierarchy of controls:** elimination → substitution → engineering → administrative → PPE (in order of effectiveness)

## Common exam trap

- **Shade number vs current:** the shade number in CSA Z94.3 is the **minimum** — you can always use a darker shade, but never lighter. Distractors give the same shade for GMAW 160 A and SMAW 400 A (they're not the same — the heavy SMAW work needs shade 12–13).
- **Arc eye symptoms:** appear **6–12 hours later**, not immediately. Distractors say "immediately" or "within 1 hour."
- **Oxygen for ventilation:** wrong, always. "Oxygen makes the air fresher" is a classic distractor. Air only for ventilation in confined spaces.
- **OCV vs welding voltage:** OCV (idle) is typically higher than arc voltage. SMAW may have 70 V OCV but 20–30 V while actively welding. The higher OCV is the shock risk.
- **Attendant entering to rescue:** the attendant does **not** enter. They alert emergency services. Untrained rescuers entering confined spaces to rescue incapacitated workers is a leading cause of confined space fatalities.
- **19.5% vs 16% O₂:** Alberta OHS requires atmospheric testing before entry when O₂ could be below **19.5%** — this is the action threshold, not 16%. At 16%, a worker rapidly loses consciousness (that's the danger level, not the "time to get worried" level).

## Practice question preview

**Q:** During GMAW welding inside a steel tank, a welder's monitor alarms showing 17% O₂. What is the correct immediate action?

A) Continue welding while increasing the ventilation blower speed  
B) Don a supplied-air respirator and continue the task  
C) Exit the confined space immediately and do not re-enter until the atmosphere is retested and acceptable  
D) Switch from CO₂ shielding gas to argon to reduce gas accumulation  

**Correct: C**

**Explanation:** At 17% O₂, the atmosphere is oxygen-deficient below the 19.5% threshold required under Alberta OHS Code Part 5 for confined space work. The correct action is immediate exit — a worker cannot safely supply their own air using an air-purifying respirator (APR) in an oxygen-deficient atmosphere (APRs do not supply oxygen). Increasing ventilation or changing shielding gas are not immediate exit substitutes. Only supplied-air breathing apparatus (SCBA or airline) allows safe re-entry once conditions are confirmed, and only after the atmosphere is retested and shown to be within acceptable limits.
