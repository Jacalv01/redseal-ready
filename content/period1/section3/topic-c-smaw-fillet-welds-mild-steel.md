# SMAW Fillet Welds on Mild Steel

This is the 30-hour practical heart of Period 1 SMAW training — and for good reason. Fillet welds on mild steel are the most common weld type in all of fabrication. Every built-up beam, every T-joint, every gusset plate, every structural attachment is fillet-welded. If you can't produce a sound, consistent, properly-sized fillet weld in all four positions, you can't work in structural fabrication.

---

## Fillet weld positions — the 1F/2F/3F/4F system

Weld positions are designated by the **AWS/CSA position numbering system**. For fillet welds, the "F" suffix means fillet.[^1] [^3]

| Position | Code | Axis orientation | Weld progression | Practical description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Flat** | 1F | Weld axis horizontal; weld face roughly horizontal | Horizontal | Both members level; T-joint with web vertical is rotated so the fillet is on top |
| **Horizontal** | 2F | Weld axis horizontal; weld face vertical | Horizontal | T-joint standing upright; fillet weld on the side |
| **Vertical** | 3F | Weld axis vertical; weld face vertical | Upward or downward | T-joint or lap joint with weld progressing up or down |
| **Overhead** | 4F | Weld axis horizontal; weld face faces downward (upward toward welder) | Horizontal | T-joint flipped upside down; welder looks up to weld |

**Visual check: which position am I in?**
Look at the weld pool. If gravity is pulling the pool DOWN into the joint (pool wants to run toward you and drip), you're approaching overhead. If the pool sits naturally in a trough, you're in flat. If the pool wants to roll sideways off the joint, you're in horizontal.

## Fillet weld terminology — key dimensions

Know these terms cold — they appear on inspection reports, engineering drawings, and exam questions.[^1] [^4]

| Term | Definition | Normal value for equal-leg fillet |
|---|---|---|
| **Leg length (w)** | Distance from the toe to the root along either face of the base metal | = specified weld size (e.g., 10 mm leg) |
| **Theoretical throat (tt)** | Shortest distance from root to hypotenuse (weld face line) | = leg × 0.707 |
| **Actual throat (ta)** | Shortest distance from root to actual weld face | ≥ theoretical throat if convex; < theoretical if concave |
| **Toe** | The junction of the weld face and the base metal surface | Must be clean, fully fused, no undercut |
| **Root** | The deepest point of the weld | Should be fully fused to both members |
| **Face** | The exposed surface of the fillet weld | Should be slightly convex to flat (code limits both convexity and concavity) |
| **Convexity** | Weld face protrudes above the toe-to-toe line | Excess convexity reduces effective throat stress transfer and is code-limited[^4] |
| **Concavity** | Weld face dips below the toe-to-toe line | Reduces actual throat below theoretical — structural deficiency |

**CSA W59 fillet weld acceptance criteria (visual inspection):**[^4]
- **Leg size:** not less than specified (no undersize)
- **Convexity:** maximum = 1 mm + 0.10 × weld size (for welds up to 8 mm); maximum = 1.5 mm + 0.10 × weld size (for welds > 8 mm)
- **Undercut:** ≤ 0.5 mm for static loads (zero for fatigue)
- **Overlap (cold lap):** none permitted
- **Cracks:** none permitted
- **Porosity:** limited by cluster size and frequency per W59 clause 5

## Electrode setup and parameters for E6010 and E7018

### E6010 (E4310) — fillet welds

| Diameter | Positions | Typical amperage range | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3/32" (2.4 mm) | All | 50–90 A | Thin plate, tight spaces |
| 1/8" (3.2 mm) | All | 75–125 A | Most common for fillet welds |
| 5/32" (4.0 mm) | 1F, 2F | 90–150 A | Use for larger fillets in flat/horiz |

**Polarity: DCEP only.**

**Arc characteristics:** stiff, digging arc. The E6010 arc is more forceful than E7018 — it "digs" into the base metal. This produces excellent fusion but requires the welder to maintain better arc length control to avoid undercut.

### E7018 (E4918) — fillet welds

| Diameter | Positions | Typical amperage range | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3/32" (2.4 mm) | All | 70–110 A | Vertical and overhead; thin plate |
| 1/8" (3.2 mm) | All | 100–165 A | Most common for structural fillet welds |
| 5/32" (4.0 mm) | 1F, 2F | 130–210 A | Larger fillets in flat/horizontal |

**Polarity: DCEP or AC.**

**Arc characteristics:** smooth, fluid weld pool, heavy slag coverage. E7018 produces a more "liquid" weld pool than E6010. The slag is heavy and full-coverage — must be completely removed between passes.

**Note:** These amperage ranges are starting points from manufacturer reference data.[^2] The actual WPS governs. Always check the electrode manufacturer's data sheet for the specific electrode diameter and confirm against the job's WPS.

## Electrode angle — the critical variables

Two angles control where the weld metal goes and how the arc forces act on the pool.[^1] [^2]

### Work angle

The angle between the electrode and the work surface, measured perpendicular to the direction of travel.

| Position | Work angle for T-joint fillet |
|---|---|
| **1F (flat)** | 45° to both members (bisects the angle of the joint) |
| **2F (horizontal)** | 30–45° toward the vertical member (lower angle directs heat to hold the pool against gravity) |
| **3F (vertical-up)** | 45° to both members; electrode slightly tilted upward |
| **4F (overhead)** | 45° to both members; electrode angled to help pool cling to joint |

**The 45° rule for equal-leg fillets:** A 45° work angle deposits equal leg lengths on both members. If your fillet is coming out with unequal legs, your work angle is off.[^1]

### Travel angle (drag angle / push angle)

The angle of the electrode in the direction of travel:

- **Drag technique (backhand):** electrode angled 10–15° away from the direction of travel (tip leads the torch body). This is standard for most SMAW fillet welding. Produces good penetration and bead control.
- **Push technique (forehand):** electrode angled 5–10° toward the direction of travel. Used in some applications; less common for SMAW fillet welds.[^1]

**Standard SMAW fillet travel angle:** 10–15° drag (pointing away from travel direction).[^1]

## Flat position (1F) fillet weld technique

The 1F position is the easiest — gravity helps you. The T-joint is rotated so the joint is at the "valley" of the T, and you're welding into a trough.[^1] [^5]

**Setup:**
- Rotate the T-joint so the web is horizontal and the fillet is in the valley (or use a jig/positioner)
- Clean the joint — no rust, oil, or paint in the weld zone
- Tack weld both ends to hold fit-up during welding

**Technique:**
1. Strike the arc at the start of the joint (back-strike method: strike ahead of the start point, then drag back to the start before the arc blows out — prevents cold starts)
2. Establish the puddle at 45° work angle, 10–15° drag
3. Travel at a steady speed to maintain consistent bead width (ideally 1× to 1.5× the electrode diameter for single-pass fillet width)
4. Fill the crater before breaking the arc — pull back slightly and pause to fill

**Single-pass vs. multi-pass:**
- Small fillet welds (≤ 3/8 in / 10 mm) are typically single-pass
- Larger fillet welds require multiple passes — use a root/hot pass first, then fill passes, then cap pass

## Horizontal position (2F) fillet weld technique

The 2F position is the most commonly encountered position in structural fab — most T-joints in an upright frame are welded in 2F. Gravity pulls the weld pool downward, causing sagging and undercut if not controlled.[^1] [^2]

**Setup:**
- T-joint standing upright; the fillet is on the side of the web plate
- The welder stands or crouches facing the joint

**The key challenge:** the weld pool wants to sag (due to gravity) and flow over the bottom toe, creating overlap/cold lap on the bottom and undercut at the top toe.

**Technique:**
- Lower your work angle slightly (30–40°) — pointing more toward the bottom member helps counteract sag
- Slightly higher travel speed than 1F — keeps the pool small and less susceptible to gravity
- E7018: the fluid pool requires careful control; short arc length helps
- E6010: the stiffer arc helps hold the pool in position

**Common defects in 2F:**
- **Undercut at top toe:** work angle too high, current too high, travel too slow
- **Overlap at bottom toe:** work angle too low, current too low, travel too slow, pool too large
- **Unequal legs:** work angle not set correctly — measure both legs with fillet gauge after each pass

## Vertical position (3F) fillet weld technique

Vertical welding fights gravity in a different way — the pool wants to run downward (drip) if you go too slow or use too much heat.[^1]

### Vertical-up (most common for structural fillet welds)

**Technique:**
- Travel upward
- Work angle: 45° to both members
- Travel angle: 0–10° drag (nearly perpendicular to the joint)
- Use a slightly lower amperage than flat/horizontal (roughly 10–15% lower) — less heat input needed because the pool has time to fuse before you move upward
- **Weave technique:** small side-to-side weave (triangular or crescent pattern) helps build leg width without excessive sagging; pause at each toe for fusion
- E7018: control the pool with small deliberate weaves; don't rush
- E6010: can use a whipping/stepping motion (lift the arc briefly, let the pool freeze slightly, then bring the arc back down) — the fast-freezing slag controls the pool in vertical better than any other technique

### Vertical-down

Faster travel, shallower penetration. Not standard for structural fillet welds governed by CSA W59 — vertical-down is usually limited to thin sheet metal and specific approved WPS applications.[^4]

---

## Overhead position (4F) fillet weld technique

**The most physically demanding position.** The weld pool is above the welder, and gravity is trying to pull the molten metal down toward your face.[^1]

**PPE critical:** overhead welding requires full face shield (not just safety glasses), leather gloves, leather sleeves, and a leather apron or welding jacket. Molten spatter will fall toward the welder.[^5] See CSA W117.2 for full PPE requirements.

**Technique:**
- Short arc length — critical. A long arc overhead allows the pool to go fluid and drip. Keep it tight.
- Lower amperage than flat (approximately 10–20% lower) — less heat = faster freezing pool
- 45° work angle to both members
- Slightly higher travel speed than flat position
- Use stringer beads in overhead — no wide weaves; stringer beads have a smaller pool that freezes faster
- E7018: reduce amperage, keep arc length very short, move steadily
- E6010: the fast-freezing slag is an asset overhead — E6010 is preferred by many experienced welders for overhead work

**Watch for:**
- Spatter falling onto the lens of your helmet — clean your lens frequently or use a proper overhead shield
- The pool dripping if you stop or slow down — keep moving at a consistent pace

## Surface welds (bead-on-plate) in flat position

Surface welds (also called "bead-on-plate" or "stringer beads on plate") are the entry skill before fillet welding. You're depositing weld metal on the surface of a flat plate — no joint, no gap, just learning to control the arc, travel speed, and bead shape.[^5]

**Purpose of bead-on-plate practice:**
- Develop a steady travel speed
- Learn to read the weld pool (size, shape, color ahead of the solidification line)
- Practice starting and stopping without cold starts or craters
- Learn the feel of the electrode burning down and maintaining arc length as the electrode shortens

**Quality indicators for surface beads:**
- Uniform width (± 1–2 mm over the bead length)
- No undercut at the edges
- Consistent reinforcement height
- Clean start (no cold blob at beginning)
- Filled crater at the end

---

## Multi-pass fillet welds

For fillet welds larger than single-electrode can produce in one pass, multiple passes are required.[^1] [^2]

**Pass sequence for a multi-pass T-joint fillet weld:**

1. **Root pass:** the first pass, establishing fusion at the root. Use smaller diameter electrode (3/32" or 1/8"). Goal: full root fusion with both base metal members.

2. **Fill passes:** subsequent passes that build up the fillet to size. Each pass is placed with correct electrode angle to achieve both toe fusion and good overlap with the previous pass (50% overlap on the previous bead is typical).

3. **Cap pass (finishing pass):** the final pass, which determines the visual appearance and final weld size. Must achieve proper weld size, no undercut at toes, correct convexity.

**Slag removal between passes:** chip and wire-brush every pass to 100% clean before the next pass. Use a wire brush, not just a chipping hammer — the chipping hammer breaks slag, the wire brush reveals the porosity, cracks, or fusion problems underneath.

## Visual inspection — what to check

After welding, visually inspect every fillet weld for:[^3] [^4]

| Item | Acceptance criterion (CSA W59 general) |
|---|---|
| **Weld size (leg length)** | Not less than the specified size — measure with fillet gauge |
| **Uniformity** | Consistent width and height along the length |
| **Cracks** | None permitted — any crack is a reject |
| **Undercut** | ≤ 0.5 mm depth (static loads); zero (fatigue loads) |
| **Overlap (cold lap)** | None permitted |
| **Convexity** | Within code limits |
| **Porosity** | Frequency and size limited per clause 5 — no piping porosity |
| **Crater fill** | End of bead filled — no crater crack |
| **Slag inclusions** | Removed before next pass; no trapped slag in final passes visible on surface |

## Numbers you need to memorize

- **Equal-leg fillet work angle: 45°** to both members[^1]
- **Standard SMAW drag angle: 10–15°** pointing away from direction of travel[^1]
- **Overhead and vertical fillet amperage: ~10–15% lower** than flat for same electrode size[^1]
- **E7018 at 1/8" (3.2 mm): 100–165 A**[^2]
- **E6010 at 1/8" (3.2 mm): 75–125 A**[^2]
- **Undercut acceptance limit (CSA W59 static): ≤ 0.5 mm**[^4]
- **Slag removal: mandatory between every pass** — 100% clean before next pass[^1]
- **Fillet leg size: never less than specified** — undersize fillet is a reject[^4]
- **1F = flat; 2F = horizontal; 3F = vertical; 4F = overhead**[^3]

## What the textbook doesn't tell you

**The weld pool colour tells you what's happening.** A bright orange pool with a shiny leading edge means the arc is properly melting the base metal ahead of the pool — good fusion. A dark orange pool with a crusty, dull appearance means the heat is insufficient or your arc length is too long and the pool is cooling too fast ahead of fusion. A white, fluid, runny pool means too much heat — slow down or reduce amperage.[^2]

**Never restart in the middle of an incomplete crater.** When you need to restart (new electrode or continuing after a stop), strike the arc approximately 1/2 inch ahead of the crater, then drag back into the crater and fill it before continuing forward. This ties in the restart, avoids cold starts (the unfused blob at the beginning of a bead), and fills the previous crater. Failing to do this leaves unfused areas at every electrode change.[^1]

**Overhead welding destroys protective gear faster than anything.** Leather gloves, sleeves, and jackets are not optional for 4F work — they are mandatory. But more practically: set your booth up correctly before you start so you can take a steady position. A welder who is awkwardly crouched under a joint will fatigue quickly, their hand will shake, and the bead will reflect that. Comfort equals consistency.[^5]

**"Walk before you run" on vertical.** The single most common apprentice failure in 3F is running the amperage too high and using too wide a weave too early. Start with the minimum acceptable amperage for the electrode diameter, use a small, controlled triangular weave, pause at each toe for 1 second, and let the pool freeze slightly between weld steps. Then gradually increase weave width as your pool control improves.

## Key terms

- **1F:** flat fillet weld position — weld axis horizontal, face upward
- **2F:** horizontal fillet weld position — weld axis horizontal, face vertical
- **3F:** vertical fillet weld position — weld axis vertical
- **4F:** overhead fillet weld position — weld axis horizontal, face downward
- **Work angle:** electrode angle perpendicular to travel direction — determines leg length equality
- **Travel angle (drag angle):** electrode angle in the direction of travel — typically 10–15° drag for SMAW
- **Fillet weld leg:** the dimension of the fillet along each member from the toe to the root
- **Theoretical throat:** leg × 0.707 for equal-leg fillet — the load-carrying dimension
- **Convexity:** excess weld metal above the toe-to-toe line — code limited
- **Concavity:** weld face below the toe-to-toe line — reduces actual throat
- **Root pass:** first pass establishing fusion at the joint root
- **Cap pass (finish pass):** final pass determining final weld appearance and size
- **Keyhole:** small opening at the root of a groove weld that indicates full root penetration
- **Back-strike start:** striking the arc ahead of the intended start point, then returning to the start before the arc extinguishes — avoids cold starts

## Common exam trap

- **"Vertical-down is preferred for structural fillet welds"** — false. Vertical-down produces shallower penetration and is NOT prequalified for most structural fillet welds under CSA W59. Vertical-up is the standard.
- **"The 4F position fillet weld requires a higher amperage than flat"** — false. Overhead fillet welds use LOWER amperage (10–15% less) to reduce pool fluidity and prevent dripping.
- **"An undersize fillet weld can be accepted if it's only 1 mm undersize"** — false. CSA W59 requires the fillet weld to be not less than the specified size. Undersize is not acceptable regardless of how small the deficiency is — repair by adding a cover pass.
- **"The work angle for a 2F horizontal fillet is 45° to both members"** — technically the ideal is 45°, but in practice 2F horizontal requires a slightly lower angle (30–40°) toward the bottom member to counteract pool sag. The exam may present the "theoretical 45°" and also ask about pool sag correction.

## Practice question preview

**Q:** A welder is producing a 2F horizontal fillet weld on a T-joint using E7018 at 120 A (1/8" electrode). The completed weld shows heavy overlap at the bottom toe and slight undercut at the top toe. Which adjustment is MOST likely to correct both defects simultaneously?

A) Increase amperage and slow travel speed  
B) Increase travel angle to 30° and use a weave pattern  
C) Lower the work angle slightly (toward the bottom member) and increase travel speed  
D) Switch to a larger electrode diameter  

**Correct: C**

**Explanation:** In 2F horizontal fillet welding, overlap at the bottom toe and undercut at the top toe are the classic signs of a work angle that is too high (too much toward the top/vertical member) combined with a pool that is too large (too slow travel speed). Lowering the work angle directs more heat/metal toward the bottom member, reducing the tendency to wash over the bottom toe while reducing the undercutting force at the top. Increasing travel speed reduces the pool size, which reduces overflow. Option A (increase amperage, slow speed) makes both problems worse. Option B (increase drag angle) doesn't address the fundamental work angle and pool-size issue. Option D (larger electrode) increases heat and pool size — opposite of what's needed.
