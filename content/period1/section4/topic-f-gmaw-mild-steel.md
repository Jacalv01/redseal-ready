# GMAW on Mild Steel

This is 30 hours of hands-on practice — the practical heart of the wire-feed section of Period 1. GMAW (MIG welding) is the process you will use more than any other in structural fabrication shops, automotive repair, pressure vessel erection, and general manufacturing. The content here bridges the theory from the previous lessons into actual weld execution: how to hold the gun, how to read the pool, how to run stringer and weave beads, and how to execute fillet and groove welds in all required positions.

---

## GMAW setup for mild steel practice — starting parameters

For Period 1 GMAW practice on mild steel, the typical starting setup is:[^1] [^2]

| Parameter | Starting value |
|---|---|
| **Wire** | ER70S-6, 0.035" (0.9 mm) or 0.045" (1.1 mm) diameter |
| **Shielding gas** | 75/25 Ar/CO₂ |
| **Gas flow rate** | 25–35 CFH (11.8–16.5 L/min) |
| **Transfer mode** | Short-circuit (for all-position practice) |
| **Polarity** | DCEP |
| **Voltage** | 17–22 V (short-circuit range — adjust to material thickness) |
| **WFS / amperage** | 150–250 in/min WFS (approximately 100–175 A) — adjust with voltage for specific thickness |
| **CTWD** | 3/8–3/4 in (10–20 mm) |
| **Travel angle** | Forehand (push) 5–15° |

**Adjust based on plate thickness:**[^1]
- 1/8" (3.2 mm) plate: lower voltage/WFS end of range — approximately 17–18 V
- 3/16"–1/4" (4.8–6.4 mm) plate: mid-range — approximately 18–20 V
- 3/8"–1/2" (9.5–12.7 mm) plate: upper short-circuit range — approximately 20–22 V, or transition toward globular range

These are reference starting points only — always verify with the electrode manufacturer's chart and adjust based on the first test bead.[^1]

## Stringer beads — the foundation

A stringer bead is a weld bead deposited with essentially no transverse (side-to-side) motion — the gun travels straight along the joint with only the forward travel motion.[^1] [^5]

**When to use stringers:**
- Root passes in groove welds (maximizes fusion at the root)
- Overhead and vertical position GMAW (small pool = better control)
- Multi-pass welds where each pass needs to be narrow and well-placed
- Any time the joint geometry doesn't allow a wide weave
- Code applications where wide weave beads are restricted by WPS

**Stringer bead technique:**
1. Position the gun at the correct work angle, CTWD, and travel angle
2. Trigger the gun — wire, arc, and gas start simultaneously
3. Hold the gun steady (no side-to-side) and travel forward at a consistent speed
4. Watch the leading edge of the weld pool — not the arc. The pool should be a consistent oval or teardrop shape. If it becomes irregular, adjust travel speed.
5. Fill the crater by slowing slightly at the end of the bead before releasing the trigger

**Reading the stringer bead:**
- **Too fast:** narrow, undercut bead — pool hasn't had time to fill the joint
- **Too slow:** wide, convex bead that rolls over the edges; cold lapping possible
- **Too high voltage (long arc):** wide, flat bead with excess spatter
- **Too low voltage (short arc):** convex bead with erratic "popping" arc sound

## Weave beads — when and how

A weave bead uses deliberate side-to-side motion as the gun travels forward — depositing a wider bead than a stringer.[^1] [^5]

**When to use weaves:**
- Building up leg width on fillet welds (when one stringer isn't wide enough)
- Fill passes in wide groove welds
- Cover/cap passes where a wider, more uniform bead is desired
- Vertical-up welding (small triangular or crescent weave to build fillet width while controlling pool)

**Types of weave patterns:**

| Pattern | Motion | Best application |
|---|---|---|
| **Side-to-side (oscillation)** | Equal motion left and right | Fill passes, flat/horizontal |
| **Crescent/C-weave** | Half-circle arcing motion | Vertical-up fillet welds |
| **Triangular weave** | Zigzag with pauses at each toe | Vertical-up groove and fillet welds |
| **Figure-8** | Wide sweeping figure-8 | Wide flat fill passes |

**Critical rule for weave beads:** Pause at each toe of the weave for approximately 0.5–1 second to allow the molten metal to flow into the toe of the joint and achieve fusion before moving back to the opposite side. This prevents undercut at the toes.[^1]

## Flat position GMAW — 1F (fillet) and 1G (groove)

### 1F — Flat fillet weld

**Setup:** T-joint positioned with the weld in the valley (rotate the joint or use a positioner). The weld faces upward.

**Technique:**
- Work angle: 45° (equal legs)
- Travel angle: 5–15° forehand (push)
- CTWD: 3/8–5/8 in (10–16 mm)
- Travel speed: steady — watch bead width

For multi-pass: root pass with stringer (smaller wire / lower parameters), fill with stringer or narrow weave, cap with weave if leg width requires it.

**Quality check:** measure both legs with a fillet gauge — must equal the specified size (not less than).[^3] [^4]

### 1G — Flat groove weld (butt joint)

**Setup:** plates in the same plane, end-to-end or with a root opening. Root opening for GMAW short-circuit on mild steel: typically 0–3 mm (no root opening for GMAW is possible at lower thicknesses; 1/8 in root opening for thicker material to achieve root penetration).[^1]

**Single-pass groove weld (thin plate, up to approximately 6 mm):**
- Can make a complete CJP weld in a single pass if the groove preparation is correct and the heat input is sufficient
- Key: watch the back side of the joint — a slight glow visible through the root gap (not burn-through) indicates full penetration

**Multi-pass groove weld (thicker plate):**
1. Root pass: small stringer, slow speed, maximize root fusion
2. Hot pass (optional): cleans up the root pass surface and ensures full sidewall fusion
3. Fill passes: stringer or weave to build up the groove
4. Cap pass: smooth weave to fill the groove to the required crown height

## Horizontal position GMAW — 2F (fillet) and 2G (groove)

### 2F — Horizontal fillet weld

**Challenges:** gravity pulls the pool downward — causes sagging, overlap at bottom toe, undercut at top toe if not corrected.

**Technique:**
- Work angle: 30–45° (lower than 45° — angled slightly toward the bottom member to counteract sag)
- Travel angle: 5–15° forehand
- CTWD: 3/8–5/8 in
- Travel speed: slightly faster than flat to keep the pool small

**Solutions to common horizontal fillet problems:**

| Problem | Correction |
|---|---|
| Overlap at bottom toe | Reduce travel speed; increase work angle toward top member |
| Undercut at top toe | Reduce voltage; increase travel speed; lower work angle |
| Unequal legs | Adjust work angle to 45° for equal legs; measure and verify with gauge |

### 2G — Horizontal groove weld (butt joint)

**Position:** plates are vertical (in a wall plane), weld axis horizontal, weld progression horizontal.

**Technique:**
- Gun angled slightly downward (10–15° below horizontal) to help control the pool and direct it into the groove
- Root pass: stringer at the root
- Fill passes: stringer beads; each bead placed slightly higher than the previous to fill the groove in steps
- Cap pass: slightly wider bead to close the groove face

## Vertical position GMAW — 3F (fillet) and 3G (groove)

### Vertical-up GMAW (standard for structural welds)

**Why vertical-up, not down:** Vertical-up provides deeper penetration and better fusion in the groove walls. Vertical-down has shallower penetration and is generally not approved for structural groove welds (unless specifically qualified by WPS).[^3]

**Key setup change for vertical-up:** Drop parameters toward the low end of short-circuit range.
- Reduce voltage 1–3 V vs. flat position (helps keep the pool smaller and less prone to sagging)
- Reduce WFS / amperage correspondingly
- Use a small, controlled weave (triangular or crescent)

**3F — Vertical fillet weld technique:**

Work angle: 45° to both members (same as flat)
Travel angle: 0–10° (nearly perpendicular to the joint — nearly straight up)
Weave technique: triangular weave — move from one toe to the centre, then the other toe, then upward. Pause 0.5–1 second at each toe.

**3G — Vertical groove weld technique:**

Root pass: stringer bead, start at the bottom of the joint, travel upward at a controlled speed. The pool should be small and not sagging.

Fill passes: stringer beads or very narrow weaves — each pass placed above the previous.
Cap pass: slightly wider weave to produce a smooth, properly profiled cap.

**The "travel angle tilted up" rule for 3G:** In vertical-up groove welding, the gun is typically angled 5–10° upward (toward the direction of travel) — this helps direct the arc into the groove root and push the pool upward rather than letting it sag.[^1]

## Bead quality checks — reading what you welded

After every practice bead or pass, inspect before the next pass:[^3] [^4]

**For stringer beads:**
- Width uniform along the bead (±2 mm acceptable for practice)
- No undercut at either edge
- Smooth start (no cold blob) and filled crater at the end
- Porosity: none visible on the surface
- Good fusion (bead adheres cleanly to both sides — not sitting on top)

**For fillet welds (measured with a fillet weld gauge):**
- Leg size ≥ specified (undersize is a reject)
- Both legs approximately equal (for equal-leg fillet)
- No undercut ≥ 0.5 mm at the toes
- No overlap/cold lap
- No cracks
- Crater filled at end of bead

**For groove welds (visual):**
- Reinforcement (crown height) within code limit (typically ≤ 3 mm above flush for CJP per CSA W59)[^4]
- Full-width cap bead with smooth transition to base metal at both toes
- No undercut at toes (≤ 0.5 mm static)
- Surface porosity: none acceptable for critical welds

## Numbers you need to memorize

- **GMAW (short-circuit) typical voltage: 17–22 V**[^1]
- **75/25 Ar/CO₂ flow rate: 25–35 CFH**[^1]
- **Standard GMAW forehand (push) travel angle: 5–15°**[^1]
- **CTWD for short-circuit GMAW: 3/8–3/4 in (10–20 mm)**[^1]
- **Fillet weld maximum undercut (CSA W59 static loads): ≤ 0.5 mm**[^4]
- **CJP groove weld reinforcement max (CSA W59): ≤ 3 mm above flush**[^4]
- **Vertical-up: use lower voltage/WFS than flat** — 1–3 V reduction[^1]
- **Pause at each toe of a weave for ~0.5–1 second** — prevents undercut[^1]
- **1F = flat fillet; 2F = horizontal fillet; 3F = vertical fillet**[^3]
- **1G = flat groove; 2G = horizontal groove; 3G = vertical groove**[^3]

## What the textbook doesn't tell you

**Read the sound, not just the arc.** An experienced GMAW welder can hear when the arc is right. Short-circuit transfer with 75/25 gas and correct voltage produces a very rapid, consistent "crackle" — like frying bacon evenly. Too low voltage produces an irregular, stuttering sound (wire sticking). Too high voltage produces a loud, harsh "splatter" sound with big droplets. Within 30 seconds of triggering, you should be able to hear if the parameters are right before you even look at the bead.

**The "gun angle cheat"** for 2F horizontal: Many experienced GMAW welders run a 30–35° work angle (lower than the textbook 45°) for horizontal fillet welds, angled toward the bottom member. This is slightly off the "balanced 45°" but it fights pool sag and produces better bottom-toe fusion in practice. The resulting fillet weld may not be exactly equal-leg — adjust with a small geometry compensation pass if needed.

**For 3G (vertical groove), always pre-clean the root pass before filling.** GMAW short-circuit root passes in a vertical groove can have rough surfaces with potential cold starts or small pores. Wire-brush or lightly grind the root pass surface before depositing fill passes. A rough root pass surface becomes a stress concentration in the finished weld.

**The pool is your only feedback.** In GMAW you don't feel the electrode burning down in your hand the way you do in SMAW. Your only real-time feedback is the weld pool shape, color, and behavior. Train yourself to watch the TRAILING edge of the pool (behind the arc, where the metal is solidifying) — consistent width there means consistent travel speed.

## Key terms

- **1G, 1F:** flat position — weld axis horizontal, face upward
- **2G, 2F:** horizontal position — weld axis horizontal, face vertical
- **3G, 3F:** vertical position — weld axis vertical
- **Stringer bead:** weld bead deposited with no transverse (side-to-side) oscillation — narrow bead
- **Weave bead:** weld bead deposited with deliberate transverse oscillation — wider bead
- **CTWD (Contact Tip to Work Distance):** total distance from end of contact tip to the workpiece surface
- **Forehand (push) technique:** gun aimed in the direction of travel — wider, shallower bead, better gas coverage
- **Root pass:** first weld pass in a multi-pass groove weld — must achieve root fusion
- **Hot pass:** second pass in a groove weld (follows root pass) — burns off residual contaminants from root pass and ensures full root fusion
- **Fill passes:** intermediate passes between root/hot pass and cap pass
- **Cap pass:** final weld pass determining surface appearance and final weld width/height
- **Undercut:** groove at the weld toe — acceptance limit ≤ 0.5 mm (CSA W59 static)
- **Reinforcement:** weld metal deposited above the base metal surface on a groove weld — limit ≤ 3 mm above flush

## Common exam trap

- **"Vertical-down (3G-D) is preferred for structural groove welds because it's faster"** — false. Vertical-down produces shallower penetration and is generally not prequalified for structural groove welds under CSA W59 or AWS D1.1. Vertical-up is the standard.
- **"For a 2F horizontal fillet, the correct work angle is 45° to both members"** — technically 45° is the theoretical ideal for equal legs, but in practice the work angle is reduced to 30–40° to counteract pool sag. The exam may test this as the "corrective" answer for pool sag problems.
- **"GMAW uses CC power source for better arc stability"** — false. GMAW uses CV (constant voltage). CC is used for SMAW and GTAW.
- **"75/25 Ar/CO₂ enables spray transfer"** — false. 75% Argon is below the ~80% minimum for spray transfer. Spray requires 90/10 Ar/CO₂ or higher Ar content.

## Practice question preview

**Q:** A GMAW welder running 75/25 Ar/CO₂ at 19 V with ER70S-6 wire completes a 2F horizontal fillet weld on 1/4 inch (6.4 mm) mild steel. The bottom toe shows heavy overlap and the top toe shows undercut. What is the MOST likely cause and correction?

A) Voltage is too high; reduce to 15 V  
B) Work angle is too high toward the vertical member; reduce the angle toward the bottom member and increase travel speed slightly  
C) Wire feed speed is too low; increase WFS by 50 in/min  
D) Shielding gas flow rate is too high; reduce to 15 CFH  

**Correct: B**

**Explanation:** The classic 2F horizontal fillet defect pattern — overlap at the bottom toe + undercut at the top toe — is caused by a work angle that is too high (aimed too much toward the vertical member). This directs the arc toward the top member, causing undercut there, while the excess weld metal rolls over the bottom toe as overlap. The correction is to lower the work angle (point more toward the horizontal/bottom member) and slightly increase travel speed to reduce pool size. Option A (reduce voltage to 15 V) would move out of the normal working range and create other problems. Option C (increase WFS) would make the pool larger and the problem worse. Option D (reduce gas flow) addresses shielding, not bead shape.
