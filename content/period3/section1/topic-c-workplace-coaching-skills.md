# Workplace Coaching Skills

When you get your journeyperson ticket, your job changes. You're no longer just a welder — you're responsible for the apprentice working beside you. The quality of their training is in your hands. Most journeypersons were never taught how to teach; they just got shown things by someone who also wasn't taught how to teach. This lesson breaks the cycle.

---

## Why This Matters — Legal and Practical

Under Alberta's Apprenticeship and Industry Training Act, a certified journeyperson working with an apprentice has specific obligations:[^2]

- **Provide adequate supervision:** you must be in a position to observe the apprentice's work and intervene if safety is at risk
- **Ensure the apprentice is performing work relevant to the trade:** an apprentice should be doing welding work, not exclusively running errands or moving material
- **Sign off on the Record of Training:** your signature on the apprentice's record book is a legal statement that they performed and demonstrated the listed competencies

Beyond the law: **how you coach directly determines whether the apprentice becomes a competent welder or a dangerous one.** The industry needs qualified welders. The fastest path is a journeyperson who can actually teach.

---

## The Four-Step Coaching Model

The most effective approach to on-the-job training follows a structured four-step cycle.[^3]

### Step 1 — Set Expectations

Before the apprentice touches any equipment:

- **Explain the task clearly:** "Today we're going to practice running the root pass on a 3G V-groove. The goal is consistent penetration with no burn-through."
- **State the standard:** "A good root pass has a slightly raised crown on the back side — about 1/16" maximum — and no undercut or cold lap at the toes."
- **Explain the safety requirements:** "Before we start, here's the PPE: full leather sleeves, shade 10 lens minimum. Keep your body to the side of the weld axis — flash burns through your sleeve if you're directly over the arc."
- **Explain what you'll be observing:** "I'm going to watch your body position, arc length, travel speed, and the sound of the arc. I'll let you run the whole pass before I give feedback."

**Why setting expectations works:** apprentices who know exactly what "good" looks like before they start are better self-monitors during the task.[^3]

### Step 2 — Demonstrate

Show the task before asking the apprentice to do it:[^1][^3]

- **Demonstrate at real speed first:** run the root pass at normal production speed. Let them see what the finished product looks like.
- **Demonstrate at slow speed with narration:** run through the key movements while talking out loud. "Watch how I hold the electrode angle — 10–15° toward the direction of travel — and listen to the arc sound. That's the sound of a correct arc length."
- **Show the checkpoints:** after the pass, show the back-side penetration bead, the cross-section if grinding is needed, and what a defect looks like vs. what an acceptable bead looks like.
- **Invite questions before the apprentice starts:** "Any questions about what you just saw? What's the first thing you're going to focus on when you start?"

### Step 3 — Observe

Let the apprentice do the task while you watch — without interrupting unless there's a safety issue:[^3]

- **Stand where you can see both the arc and the apprentice's body position**
- **Watch for:** electrode angle drift, arc length changes (audible — too long sounds like crackling, correct sounds smooth and steady), puddle control, travel speed consistency, body position
- **Take notes mentally** (or on a card) of what went well and what needs improvement
- **Do NOT correct mid-task** unless:
  - Safety is at risk (flash, fire, crush)
  - The error will compound and make the coupon unsalvageable (e.g., running way too fast and creating a cold joint that will fail)
  - The apprentice asks you to stop

**Why observe without interrupting:** constant correction during the task prevents the apprentice from developing self-monitoring. They become dependent on the journeyperson's voice instead of their own senses.[^3]

### Step 4 — Provide Feedback

Structured feedback after the task is the most powerful tool you have.[^1][^3]

#### The feedback sequence

1. **Ask first:** "How do you think that went? What felt right? What felt off?"
   - This develops self-assessment — the most important skill for a welder working alone
   - Often the apprentice already knows what they did wrong

2. **Reinforce the specific things that were correct:** "Your electrode angle was rock-solid for the first half. That's where I want to see it every pass."
   - Be specific — not "good job" — the apprentice needs to know WHICH behaviors to repeat

3. **Identify the one most important thing to improve:** "The one thing I want you to work on next time: arc length. When you speeded up in the middle, your arc stretched out. Listen for that crackling sound — that's your signal to close up."
   - One improvement point at a time. Giving 5 things to fix at once is overwhelming and produces no change.

4. **Demonstrate the corrected behavior:** if the correction is technical, show it again immediately while describing it

5. **Have them try again:** repeat the cycle immediately with the correction in mind

#### Feedback language matters

| Instead of... | Say... |
|---|---|
| "You're doing it wrong" | "Here's what I noticed — try this adjustment" |
| "That's terrible" | "The root is cold at the start — let's look at why" |
| "Watch me again" | "Let me show you what a correct arc length looks like" |
| "You're not listening" | "Let me explain this differently — here's what's happening physically" |

## Safety Oversight — Non-Negotiable

As a journeyperson coaching an apprentice, your first responsibility is safety:[^4]

- **PPE verification:** check that the apprentice is fully geared (correct shade lens, gloves, sleeves, footwear) BEFORE they strike an arc
- **Confined space:** never let an apprentice work in a confined space without a trained entry supervisor present — this is not a task to practice with a new apprentice unless you are a trained confined space entry supervisor yourself
- **Hot work permits:** if the shop requires a hot work permit, the apprentice must see you complete it — learning permit compliance is part of the apprenticeship
- **Immediate stop authority:** if you observe any unsafe act, stop the work immediately, explain the hazard, and correct it before proceeding. This is both your right and your obligation under the OHS Act.

---

## Giving Feedback on Safety Violations

Safety feedback must be direct and immediate — this is the one time you DO interrupt:[^4]

- **Stop the work:** "Stop. Put down the electrode."
- **State the hazard:** "You don't have your gloves on and you're holding that hot metal."
- **Correct it:** "Put on your gloves. Always. No exceptions."
- **Explain the consequence:** "A burn to your hand can put you off work for weeks. The gloves are there for a reason."
- **Check understanding:** "What are you going to do differently every time from here on?"

Never make safety feedback personal — it's about the behavior, not the person.

---

## Numbers you need to memorize

- **Alberta journeyperson-to-apprentice ratio:** 1:1 mandatory supervision for first-period apprentices (verify current AIT regulations — ratios may vary by employer permit)[^2]
- **Feedback principle:** one improvement point at a time — not five[^3]
- **Feedback sequence:** Ask → Reinforce specific positives → One improvement → Demonstrate → Repeat[^3]

---

## What the textbook doesn't tell you

**Patience is a skill, not a personality trait.** Good coaches are not born patient — they practice restraint. Every time you resist grabbing the electrode out of the apprentice's hand and doing it yourself, you're building their competence and your coaching skill simultaneously.[^3]

**Show-and-tell beats tell-only every time.** Telling an apprentice that their arc length is wrong has almost no effect. Showing them what the weld bead looks like when the arc is too long — and then what it looks like correct — fixes the problem in one session.[^1]

**Journal your observations.** After coaching sessions, a brief note on what the apprentice did well and what needs work helps you track progress over weeks. "Improved arc length consistency since last Tuesday" is useful feedback context. Without notes, it's easy to focus on the same issue for months without recognizing progress.[^3]

**The apprentice who seems unteachable is usually a coaching failure, not a learning failure.** If the apprentice can't do a task after three attempts with the same explanation, the explanation is wrong — not the apprentice. Change how you're teaching, not how you're judging.[^3]

## Key terms

- **Coaching:** a structured method of developing skills through demonstration, observation, and feedback
- **Expectations:** the clear statement of the standard and behavior before a task begins
- **Demonstration:** showing the correct technique at real and reduced speed before the apprentice attempts it
- **Observation:** watching the apprentice perform without interruption (except for safety)
- **Feedback:** structured communication that reinforces correct behavior and specifies one improvement
- **Self-assessment:** the apprentice's ability to evaluate their own work — the goal of progressive coaching
- **Journeyperson obligation:** the legal and professional responsibility of a certified tradesperson to supervise and develop apprentices under Alberta AIT and the OHS Act

---

## Common exam trap

- **The Red Seal RSOS does include mentoring (A-6.02)** even though it is weighted 0 questions in the exam blueprint. Knowledge of mentoring principles may appear as context in questions about supervisory responsibility or A-3 safety tasks.
- **Feedback should be specific, not general.** "Good job" is not feedback. "Your electrode angle was consistent for the first pass" is feedback.
- **Interrupting mid-task should be reserved for safety** — not for technique corrections that can wait until the pass is complete.
- **As a journeyperson, you are not just morally responsible — you are legally responsible** for the safety of the apprentice working under your supervision. The OHS Act places this obligation on employers and supervisors, which includes journeypersons in supervisory roles.

## Practice question preview

**Q:** A journeyperson welder is coaching an apprentice on 3G root passes. The apprentice runs a pass with inconsistent arc length but finishes without a safety incident. What is the best next step for the journeyperson?

A) Take over and run the next pass correctly without comment  
B) Tell the apprentice they did it wrong and explain all five things to improve  
C) Ask the apprentice how they think the pass went, then address the one most important issue — arc length — with a demonstration  
D) Have the apprentice observe the journeyperson run ten more passes before attempting again

**Correct: C**

**Explanation:** Effective coaching follows the Ask → Reinforce → One Improvement → Demonstrate → Repeat sequence. Asking first develops self-assessment. Limiting feedback to the one most critical issue prevents overwhelm. A demonstration of the correct behavior is more effective than verbal description alone. Option A deprives the apprentice of a learning opportunity. Option B overwhelms with too many correction points. Option D is excessive observation time — the apprentice learns by doing, not by watching repetitively without feedback.
